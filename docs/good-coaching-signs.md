# What Good Coaching Looks Like

How can a player or a family tell whether a team is being coached well? Not from the scoreboard alone. Losing does not automatically mean poor coaching, and a team can win while developing poorly. Direct play is not automatically bad soccer either: a longer pass with a clear purpose can be exactly right. But at 13 and 14, a team should be learning much more than "get the ball and kick it forward." Look for evidence of learning, week after week.

## The freeze test

**Could I freeze this game at a random moment and see evidence of coaching even if I couldn't hear the coach?**

Coaching shows up in what players do without being told: where they stand, when they move, what they look at, and how they react when the ball is lost. On film, pause at a random moment and look at the whole team, not just the ball (see the [Film Review Guide](film-review-guide.md)). One freeze proves little. Look for the pattern.

## Signs of a well-coached team

No team shows every sign all the time. Ask which column the team lives in most.

| Better coached | Less coached |
|---|---|
| Scan before receiving | Scan after the ball arrives |
| Triangles and staggered heights | Flat lines |
| Ball carrier has several options | Ball carrier is isolated |
| Players move after passing | Players watch after passing |
| Purposeful progression | Kicking it forward |
| Coordinated pressing | Individual chasing |
| Intelligent risk is tolerated | Players fear mistakes |
| Practices look like the team's game problems | Generic drills |
| Players understand why | Players memorize where to stand |

## Signs of a good development environment

Game day shows only part of the picture. Look at the week too.

- Most practice looks like soccer (opponents, direction, decisions), with little standing in lines.
- Players can say what problem today's practice taught them to solve.
- Every player knows her current priority and how playing time is earned.
- Coaches explain why, offer choices, and ask for player input.
- Effort and improvement are noticed, and good decisions that fail are not punished.
- Players experience more than one role.
- Coaches check on well-being, not only performance.

**Warning signs:** intimidation, public blame, rivalry between teammates, players afraid to ask for the ball.

## Questions a player can ask herself

- **"What soccer problem did today's practice teach me to solve?"** Escaping pressure, switching the field, defending a 2v2. If players cannot answer week after week, that is meaningful evidence.
- **"Where could I go right now that would make me useful?"** Well-coached players ask this all game, with and without the ball.
- **"Will this environment make me a better player?"** Judge it over a season, not a weekend. Game roles hand out touches unevenly, so training, small-sided games, and rotation should give her the repetitions her match role does not.
- **"How do I make sure I still become the player I want to become?"** Her team role is where she helps the team today. Her development role is the complete player she is becoming. See [Team role and development role](team-culture-and-leadership.md#team-role-and-development-role).

If the answers raise concerns, start with a conversation, not a verdict (see [Parent Partnership](parent-partnership.md)). Coaches can use every question here as a self-check: the best coaching progressively makes the coach less necessary.
