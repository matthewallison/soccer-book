# Injuries, Recovery, and Return to Play

Injuries are part of sports. We should prepare for them without becoming afraid of them. Our goals are to:

- protect the player's health
- support the injured player emotionally
- keep her connected to the team
- communicate clearly with parents and coaches
- respect appropriate medical guidance
- help the player return without unnecessary fear
- create a team culture that cares about people more than availability for the next game

## Health comes first

A player's long-term health matters more than a practice, tournament, league game, or season. Players should never feel pressure to hide an injury because they are afraid of losing playing time, disappointing teammates, or appearing weak. Reporting an injury honestly is responsible. Following treatment and rehabilitation is responsible. Taking the time needed to recover is responsible.

Sore muscles the day after a hard or new workout are common and ease with rest. Pain that starts while you play is different. Stop and tell a parent and your coach if:

- something starts to hurt while you play, especially an old injury
- you are limping or moving differently to protect it
- it is swollen, or you can't move it normally
- the pain is very bad, getting worse, or not getting better with rest

A limp, swelling, not being able to move it normally, very bad pain, or pain or swelling that gets worse or does not go away after a few days means see a doctor. Growing players: also see [Home Development Menu: Pain rule](home-development.md#pain-rule).

After a blow to the head or body, tell your coach right away, and come out if you feel dazed, dizzy, or "not right." Watch for these in yourself or a teammate: dazed or confused, slow to answer, unsteady, a change in mood, headache, dizziness, nausea, vision problems, bothered by light or noise, or feeling foggy. Any of these: come out, and unless a health care professional on site clears you, you are done for the day. **When in doubt, sit out.** A concussion follows its own step-by-step plan led by your health care provider, with no contact, heading, or full training until you are cleared. See [Parent Partnership: Health signals](parent-partnership.md#health-signals) and [Coaching Game Model: Returning after a concussion](coaching-game-model.md#returning-after-a-concussion).

## What toughness means

We respect the toughness of athletes. But toughness does not mean ignoring an injury. Toughness can mean:

- telling the truth when something hurts
- accepting that you may temporarily be unable to play
- doing rehabilitation consistently
- staying mentally engaged while injured
- supporting teammates while you recover
- dealing patiently with setbacks
- returning with courage when you are ready

We should respect the work an injured teammate is doing to return. We should never pressure her to prove toughness by playing before she is ready.

## Communication after an injury

Parents should communicate with the coach after a meaningful injury. When appropriate, please share:

- the general nature and extent of the injury
- any medical restrictions that affect soccer
- the expected recovery process or timeline, if one is known
- when the player may begin practicing again
- any limitations during the return process

The purpose is not for the coach to make medical decisions. The purpose is for the coach to understand how to support the player safely. Medical diagnosis, rehabilitation, and clearance decisions belong with the player, parents, and appropriate health care professionals. The coach should respect medical restrictions completely. Within those limits, the coach plans her training and game minutes.

She should have a voice in her own plan. The details of her injury belong to her and her family. The coach shares with the team only what she and her family agree to, and the adults who run practice are told her limits. Coach messages to her about the injury include a parent, like every coach message.

## Take care of your teammates

When a teammate gets hurt, let her know that she matters beyond what she contributes on the field. Send her a message. Ask how she is doing. Tell her you are thinking about her. Tell her you are looking forward to having her back. Keep including her.

A simple message such as "Hope you're doing okay. We miss you and can't wait to have you back" can mean a great deal to an injured teammate. Players should not disappear socially from the team simply because they temporarily cannot play.

## The injured player is still part of the team

When appropriate and comfortable for the player, injured players should remain connected. That might include:

- attending games or practices
- participating in team meetings
- watching film
- helping observe tactical problems
- encouraging teammates
- learning a new position or concept
- staying part of the team socially

None of this should become an obligation when the player needs rest, treatment, school time, or emotional space. The goal is connection, not guilt.

## Do not make the injury her identity

Ask about the player, not only the injury. An injured athlete can quickly feel as though every conversation begins with "How's the knee?" or "When are you coming back?" Check in, but also talk to her normally. She is still the same teammate.

Feeling sad, angry, frustrated, or left out after an injury is common. If those feelings are not easing or are getting worse, or if she pulls away from friends or her sleep or eating changes a lot, players tell a parent and coaches tell her family. Talking with a counselor or doctor is part of recovery, just like rehab. If she talks about hurting herself, get help right away: call or text 988, or call 911 in an emergency.

## Returning can be mentally difficult

Being medically recovered and feeling completely fearless are not always the same thing. A player returning from injury may hesitate during:

- contact
- sprinting
- cutting
- jumping
- tackling
- striking the ball
- repeating the movement associated with the injury

That does not necessarily mean she lacks courage. Feeling nervous and excited at the same time is common. Confidence may need to be rebuilt through successful repetitions. The coach should allow the player to regain trust in her body without constantly drawing attention to the injury. If fear is not fading after she is cleared, she should tell a parent so her provider knows.

## Return progressively

The return should move from controlled situations toward full competition as appropriate.

A typical path, set with her health care provider: controlled work on her own → team drills without contact → full training → short game minutes → full games. She moves up when she handles each step well, not just because a date arrives. If pain or swelling comes back, she steps back and her family checks with her provider. Stepping back is part of the process, not a failure. Short minutes at first are part of the plan, not a judgment. After a serious knee injury, such as a torn ACL, returning to cutting and pivoting sports like soccer often takes a year or more, and her medical team sets the timeline. The chance of another ACL injury is highest in the first year after surgery, so waiting until she is truly ready is the tough choice.

A player may need to rebuild:

- movement confidence
- timing
- fitness
- ball comfort
- decision speed
- contact confidence
- trust in the injured area

The goal is not simply "Can she survive a game?" The better question is "Can she play freely and confidently again?"

## Do not be afraid of the game

Once a player has appropriately recovered and is cleared to return, we want her gradually to trust herself again. We do not want the injury to permanently teach fear. The player should eventually be able to think: "I am prepared. I am strong. I can play normally again." The team and coach should help reinforce that confidence. Do not constantly remind the returning player that she was injured. Do not treat her as fragile once she is ready. Support her return to being an athlete.

## Teammates should respect the return process

Do not question why a teammate is still out. Do not tell her she should be playing. Do not tease her about being injured. Do not compare recovery times. Do not assume that because two injuries sound similar, their recovery should be the same. Do not pass on details or guesses about her injury, in person or online. Instead:

**Support. Encourage. Welcome her back.**

## Coaches should never reward hidden injuries

A player who plays through something she should have reported should not become the model of toughness for the team. We want players to trust that they can tell us when something is wrong. That trust protects athletes. **Telling us early is never held against you.** Sitting out while you are hurt is not a punishment. Coaches thank a player for speaking up, every time. Parents: say the same thing at home.

## When a player returns

Welcome her back normally. Do not make the first mistake, missed tackle, lost sprint, or moment of hesitation into evidence that she came back too soon. She may need time to regain rhythm. Encourage her to focus on the same principles she used before the injury: the practice mantra (**SCAN. TALK. INTENTION. FASTER.**) and the game mantra (**CALM MIND. FAST SOCCER.**). And when the first difficult physical moment arrives:

**WANT THE MOMENT.**

Not because she should ignore fear or pain, but because once she is healthy, cleared, and ready, that moment is part of rebuilding confidence.

## The team standard

We care about our teammates when they are playing. We care about them when they are injured. We care about them while they recover. And we care enough about them not to rush them back.

**Health first. Stay connected. Recover completely. Return confidently.**
