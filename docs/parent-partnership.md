# Parent Partnership

Parents are partners in a player-first development environment. Players do the playing and, more and more, their own thinking and talking. Coaches teach, plan, and set roles. Families make the rest possible: rides, meals, sleep, and the steady message that she is loved whatever the score. To judge the coaching itself, see [What Good Coaching Looks Like](good-coaching-signs.md).

## Player first

**Player first. Parent second. Safety immediately.**

- Questions about role, minutes, or development start with the player. She books office hours, and you help her prepare (see [Asking for opportunities](team-culture-and-leadership.md#asking-for-opportunities)).
- If you still have a concern about a match, wait 24 hours, then ask the coach for a conversation.
- Any safety, health, or welfare concern is raised right away, with no waiting period. For abuse or an emergency, see "Communication and safeguarding" below.

## On the sideline

- Cheer for the whole team, especially effort and brave ideas.
- Leave instruction to the coaches. Two voices giving different directions confuse players.
- Leave officials alone.
- Never criticize a player who misses or declines a penalty. After any mistake, cheer the next play.

**Talk about what bodies can do, never how they look.**

Praise strong, fast, balanced, and brave. No comments about weight, shape, or appearance from coaches, parents, or teammates.

## After the game

- **"I love watching you play."**
- Let her start the soccer conversation: "Want to talk about it now or later?"
- Save critique for later. Keep it honest and short, and use the team's words: What did you see? Next play.
- No player carries a result alone. Don't replay one moment as the reason the game was lost.

## Helping at home

Use the team's words (scan, carry, next play), offer rather than require, and leave new tactical instruction to the coach. The [Home Development Menu](home-development.md) has short, optional sessions she can choose from. Count the whole week: all organized sport adds up. The National Athletic Trainers' Association recommends at least two days a week free of organized training and games. Protect sleep; teens need 8–10 hours ([Recovery basics](home-development.md#recovery-basics)).

## If something seems to be missing

Every environment teaches some things well and others less. If you see a pattern over weeks (the same game problem never addressed, no development feedback, one role all season, or fear after mistakes), help her notice it without turning it into a verdict on the coach. Ask what she gets lots of reps at and what she gets almost none of. Help her fill gaps with the [Home Development Menu](home-development.md) and pickup games, and help her prepare a good question for office hours. Keep criticism of the coach out of her hearing. See [What Good Coaching Looks Like](good-coaching-signs.md#if-something-seems-to-be-missing).

## Health signals

**Fuel and periods.** Growing athletes need enough total food, including iron, calcium, and vitamin D. Eating too little for the training load (low energy availability) harms both health and performance. Once periods have started, going about 3 months or more without one is not a normal part of hard training. The same goes for no period by age 15, or within 3 years after her breasts started to develop. See a doctor.

**Head injuries.** After any blow to the head or body, a player who seems dazed, unsteady, or "not right" comes out now. Unless a health care professional on site clears her, she is done for the day.

**When in doubt, sit her out.**

Returning after a concussion is a medical process led by her health care provider, not a coaching decision: step by step, and cleared (in writing where your state requires it) before contact, heading, or full training. Tell the coach about any concussion, meaningful injury, or medical restriction, even outside soccer, so she can be supported safely ([Injuries, Recovery, and Return to Play](injuries-and-return-to-play.md)).

## Communication and safeguarding

**Club safeguarding policy comes first.** Every team adult, parent volunteers included, keeps SafeSport training and background screening current and follows club and US Club Soccer rules. If this handbook and club policy differ, club policy wins. Check it each season. Safety or abuse concerns are reported immediately, never saved for office hours: 911 in an emergency, local authorities for suspected child abuse, and the U.S. Center for SafeSport (833-587-7233 or its online form).

**Open. Observable. About soccer.**

- Team news and film questions go through one club-approved team app. Parent accounts are the main contact, and players join only if the app's age rules allow.
- Every coach message to a player includes her parent, even if she writes first. No social-media DMs or disappearing-message apps. A parent's written request to stop electronic contact is honored.
- Office hours: the coach stays 15 minutes after every practice, and a player can book up to 5 minutes, in person or through the team app with a parent included.
- Talks happen at the field, in view of others. **Out of earshot is fine. Out of sight is not.** Parents may listen in. No talks in cars or closed rooms; no player waits alone with a coach for her ride.
- In carpools, no player rides alone with an unrelated adult without advance written parent permission, and drivers pick up their own child first and drop her off last when possible.
- Please take SafeSport's free parent training each year. It explains what good communication and supervision look like and how to raise a concern ([Sources and Further Reading](further-reading.md)).

## Film

**Team film stays with the team.** Watch game film through the team's private link or app, and don't repost it publicly. No recording in locker rooms, restrooms, or changing areas. If you want a clip of your daughter taken down, ask, and it comes down. See [Sharing film safely](film-review-guide.md#sharing-film-safely).
