# Home Development Menu

Home work should make a player **more capable, not more tired**.

This is an optional menu, not a requirement to complete everything. In a heavy team week, recovery may be the best choice.

A useful approach is to choose 2–3 short items and work with quality.

**Count the whole week.**

Team practices, games, other sports, and structured training sessions all add up. The National Athletic Trainers' Association recommends:

- no more hours of organized sport per week (all sports together) than your age in years
- at least two days a week free of organized training and games

Going over that is a warning sign. Relaxed free play (juggling, pickup, kicking around) is different from adding another hard session.

**Rest is part of the plan.**

- On at least 1–2 of your days free of organized training, skip soccer completely: no home menu, juggling, or pickup. Other active play is fine.
- Build real breaks from soccer into the year.

## 1. Wall work: primary first-touch training

Wall work is our main first-touch training because the ball comes back with pace.

### Useful patterns

- firm pass → receive across your body → play with the opposite foot
- one-touch set → move to a new angle → receive again
- weak-foot receiving and passing
- first touch away from pressure
- first touch into a carry
- one-touch and two-touch combinations
- vary the distance to change the return speed
- put two cone gates behind you and choose an exit before the ball returns

**Add a look.** On some reps, check a shoulder while the ball travels to the wall. A partner behind you can hold up fingers or point to a gate. Receive toward that exit.

Pure reps build technique. Reps with a look and a choice are closer to the game. Use both.

**Juggling helps build touch. Receiving develops usable touch.**

A good first touch is not stopping the ball. It is using the arriving ball to begin the next action.

## 2. Juggling

Juggling is useful for:

- coordination
- comfort with different surfaces
- foot-eye control
- weak-foot familiarity
- general ball mastery

Use it as a supplement rather than the only touch work.

## 3. Triangle-cone dribbling

Place three cones in a triangle.

Work around them with:

- inside and outside touches
- sole pulls
- V-cuts
- drag-pushes
- Cruyff turns
- L-turns
- changes of speed
- weak-foot actions
- improvised moves

Do not repeat only one pattern.

Add the team deception principle:

**Look like you're changing your mind.**

The goal is speed plus creativity.

## 4. Small-space keep-away

With a parent, sibling, or friend:

- use a small box
- protect the ball
- use changes of direction
- shield
- escape pressure
- use deception

Even a short 1v1 keep-away session adds decision-making that cone work cannot provide.

Keep-away is a game skill, not just a drill. You do not need to be a flashy dribbler. In a game, when nobody is open forward, think:

**I can protect it, turn away, carry sideways, or go backward, and keep it.**

**Pickup counts.** Keep-away, futsal, and small games with friends build reading and decision-making. Play for fun adds to team training; it does not replace it.

## 5. Passing and longer ball work

Get comfortable with more than short rondo distances. In games, when we escape pressure and the field opens, the problem has changed: a longer pass into space can be the best solution.

When space allows, practice:

- firm 10–20 yard ground passes
- longer driven ground passes (20–40 yards)
- diagonals
- receiving a longer pass while moving
- controlling the ball into the next action

The goal is intentional range, not simply kicking far. A longer pass with a clear purpose is not a clearance.

**Purposeful distance, not hopeful distance.**

## 6. Weak-foot minimum

Every technical session should include some weak-foot work:

- passing
- receiving
- carrying
- turning
- finishing when appropriate

**Weak-foot work does not cost your strong foot.** In one study, players who trained only their weak foot for 8 weeks improved both feet.

Even men's World Cup players rarely use their weaker foot. The goal is a real option when the picture calls for it.

## 7. Jump rope

Useful for:

- rhythm
- lower-leg coordination
- springy, reactive ankles
- general foot activity

Keep it short and crisp. It counts as jumping, so the "How much" and "Pain rule" guidance in section 8 applies to it too.

## 8. Low-level plyometrics

Plyometrics are quick, springy jumps and hops that train your legs to push off and land well.

Examples:

- pogo jumps
- line hops
- snap-downs
- controlled two-leg jumps
- low-volume single-leg hops when technique is sound

### Priorities

- **Land soft and quiet.** Stick the landing before the next jump.
- **Knees stay in line over the toes.** Don't let them cave in.
- On cuts: plant, sink the hips, push off with the knee over the foot.
- Quality over quantity.

### How much

**Count good reps, not minutes.**

- A few short sets of crisp reps per drill, with full rest between sets.
- Jump first in the session, while fresh.
- Stop when landings get loud or knees cave in.
- Learn a drill at team training before doing it at home.
- Jump rope counts as jumping. In a heavy team week, jumping is the first thing to cut.

### Pain rule

Heel pain, or pain at the bump just below the kneecap, is common in growing players.

- If it hurts during or after jumping, skip jump rope and hops for now and choose ball work instead.
- Jump on grass or turf, not concrete, in well-cushioned shoes.
- Tell a parent and your coach.
- A limp, or pain that is making soccer no fun, means see a doctor.

For any pain or injury beyond this kind of growth pain, tell a parent and your coach, and see [Injuries, Recovery, and Return to Play](injuries-and-return-to-play.md).

## 9. Strength basics: calves, hips, hamstrings, trunk

Examples:

- straight- and bent-knee calf raises, then single-leg raises and holds near the top
- squats and lunges (knees in line over the toes)
- single-leg balance and slow single-leg squats
- front and side planks
- partner Nordic hamstrings at the FIFA 11+ beginner level, only after learning them at team training

1–3 sets of 6–15 controlled reps (far fewer for Nordics), 2–3 times a week, not on back-to-back days. Our injury-prevention warm-up (FIFA 11+ or a similar program) uses many of these same exercises, so warm-up days count toward your 2–3 strength days.

**Technique first, then add reps.** Stop the set when form breaks down.

## 10. Acceleration and change of direction

Short, high-quality work is enough.

Examples:

- 5–10 yard accelerations
- lateral starts
- crossover starts
- reaction starts
- short planned cuts
- short reactive cuts with a partner cue

On every cut, use the priorities in section 8. If heel or knee pain shows up, the pain rule in section 8 applies to sprints too.

Real agility involves reacting to information. Ladders can help rhythm and coordination, but should not be treated as the main agility method.

## 11. Film study counts as development

Ten focused minutes of film can be more useful than adding another hard physical session.

Use the [Film Review Guide](film-review-guide.md).

## 12. Mental reps

3–5 minutes, especially the day before a game:

- picture your role in the four moments
- rehearse your response after a mistake: your [team reset](team-culture-and-leadership.md#team-reset), then next play
- pick one cue from your [position card](positions/)

Mental reps add to real practice. **They do not replace it.**

## Sample short sessions

### Technical 15 minutes

- 7 min wall work
- 5 min triangle dribbling
- 3 min juggling or weak foot

### Athletic 12 minutes

- jump rope in a few short, crisp sets with rest (about 3 min)
- 2–3 short sets of pogos or line hops, full rest
- 3 min strength basics
- 3 min short acceleration or movement quality

### Mixed 20 minutes

- about 4 min jumping and coordination: a few short sets of jump rope, or 2–3 short sets of hops, full rest
- 8 min wall work
- 5 min dribbling
- 3 min film or visualization

For visualization, replay one receiving moment from your last game in your head: check both shoulders, spot the pressure and the space, choose your touch. Then replay it again with a better choice.

## Recovery basics

**Sleep is training.** Aim for 8–10 hours. Teen athletes who usually sleep less than 8 hours get injured more often. When time is short, sleep beats an extra session.

Rest days and weekly hours are covered at the start of this menu ("Count the whole week" and "Rest is part of the plan").

Parents: fueling, health signals, and how to help at home are covered in [Parent Partnership](parent-partnership.md#helping-at-home).

## Final principle

Extra work should sharpen skills and confidence without undermining team training, school, sleep, or recovery.

**Short, focused, high-quality work beats tired volume.**
