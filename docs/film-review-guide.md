# Film Review Guide

The purpose of film study is not to decide whether you "played well." It is to learn how to see the game more clearly and become your own coach.

## The most important rule

**Pause before you know the outcome.**

If you wait until after the play ends, it is too easy to judge the decision only by what happened.

A good decision can fail because of execution.
A poor decision can succeed because of luck.

Even professionals get fooled. In one study, shots that hit the post and went in earned players better ratings than similar shots that hit the post and stayed out.

**Judge the choice, not the bounce.**

## Review sequence

For each useful clip:

1. **Pause before the action.**
2. **Scan the picture.** What information should the player know?
3. **List the realistic options.** Try to find more than one.
4. **Play each option forward 2–4 actions.** What might happen next?
5. **Find one non-obvious option.** Could deception or creativity change the picture?
6. **Choose.** Commit to the option you would pick before you press play.
7. **Watch the play.** What actually happened?
8. **Judge decision and execution separately.**
9. **Write down one useful lesson.**

## Questions to ask

### Without the ball

- If I did not touch the ball, could I still have influenced the play?
- Did my position create an option, pull a defender, or protect dangerous space?

### Before receiving

- Did I scan early enough?
- Where was pressure coming from?
- Where was my best support?
- Was there open space I could have carried into?
- What did my body shape allow me to see?

### In possession

- Did I recognize the best advantage?
- Did I pass simply because someone was close?
- Could I have carried farther?
- Was a switch available?
- Could I have fixed a defender first?
- Did my pass help the receiver's next action?
- Could deception have created a better option?

### After passing

- Did I move again?
- Did I become another outlet?
- Did I create space for someone else?
- Did I prepare for a possible turnover?

### Defending

- Did I protect the most dangerous space?
- Could I see the ball and the dangerous opponent or space?
- Who pressured the ball, and who covered her?
- Did I step, delay, cover, or recover appropriately?
- Could I have intercepted instead of simply following my player?
- If recovering, did I use a good pursuit angle or simply chase from behind?
- If we could not win it immediately, did we get compact and protect the middle?

### Transition

After losing it:

- Could we counterpress?
- If not, did I recover immediately?
- Was I already in a useful rest-defense position (placed to stop a counterattack while we still had the ball)?

After winning it:

- Did I scan forward first?
- Was there a real breakaway opportunity?
- If not, did we secure possession?

### Team shape

- Did we form triangles, or stand in flat lines?
- Who gave width? Who gave depth?
- Did players move before the pass, or only after it?
- Was there a backward option, and could it have opened something forward?
- When nothing good was on, did we keep the ball?
- When was dribbling better than passing?

## Watch away from the ball

Some of the most important learning happens where the ball is not.

Pick a player and watch only her for 5–10 minutes.

Notice:

- how often she scans
- when she moves before the pass
- how she creates angles
- when she stays wide or moves inside
- how she reacts after passing
- what she does on the weak side
- how she prepares for transitions
- how much she influences play without touching the ball

## Review one theme at a time

Do not try to analyze an entire game at once.

Choose 6–10 useful clips around one theme.

New to film study? Start with these themes, in this order:

1. scanning and awareness
2. first touch
3. supporting the player on the ball
4. receiving and playing through pressure
5. off-ball movement
6. defending and defensive positioning

Then add themes such as:

- carrying into space
- switching play
- pursuit angles
- transition reaction
- deception
- weak-side behavior

## Three-part self-review

After a game, identify:

### One thing I did well

Something to continue and strengthen.

### One thing I need to improve

A specific behavior, not a vague judgment.

Bring your one thing to improve to the next team film share at practice, where each player names one thing from her own game.

### One solution I want to try

A concrete action for the next practice or game.

Take your one solution into the next practice, then look for it again on the next film.

**Film trains what you look for. Practice and games turn it into speed.**

## Simple game tally

From one half of film, count:

- times I received the ball
- receptions with at least one scan first (a head turn away from the ball in the 5 seconds before the first touch)
- receptions facing forward or on the half-turn (side-on, so you can see forward and back)
- passes or carries that broke a line (got the ball past a line of opponents)
- times I moved to a new angle after passing
- times I won or blocked the ball by reading the play
- times I asked for the ball

Skip clips where you cannot clearly see the head. Far-away team-camera video often cannot show it. You often cannot hear calls on it either, so count only the asks for the ball you can see (pointing, showing for it) or hear.

Compare scanning like with like. Everyone scans less under tight pressure, near the touchline, and near goal. Center midfielders (the pivots and the 10) usually scan the most and forwards the least, so compare your own clips from the same position and situation.

**Compare with your own last game, not with teammates.**

Record your tally and your three-part self-review on the [Game Reflection](templates/game-reflection.md) sheet.

## Watch the next developmental level

Professional matches are useful, but also watch soccer just 2–3 years above your age group. It shows what players only a little older are expected to do. For U13–U14, start with U15–U16.

Starting in 2026–27, ECNL, ECNL RL, Girls Academy, and most U.S. youth leagues group players by **school year (Aug 1–Jul 31)**, not birth year. League events use U-ages, so search by U-age:

- "ECNL girls U16 full match"
- "ECNL RL girls U16 full match"
- "ECNL girls national playoffs U16"
- "Girls Academy National Finals U16"
- a strong club name plus "U16 girls"

Swap "U16" for "U15" in any of these searches.

**Be careful with birth-year titles.** A "2010" video from 2025–26 shows U16 players. Today the same label can mean U16 or U17 players. Add the season (for example "2025-26") when you search by birth year.

Where to find full games:

- **ECNLTV** (paid; ECNL and ECNL RL games, plus national playoffs with replays)
- **Girls Academy on YouTube** (National Finals livestreams)
- club channels that post their own full games

Services change every season. Check each league's website first. Links are in [Sources and Further Reading](further-reading.md).

Prefer **full matches**, not highlights.

Highlights teach moments. Full matches teach soccer.

## What to compare across levels

Compare four levels using the same questions:

1. our match
2. a good U15–U16 ECNL RL match
3. a top U15–U16 ECNL match
4. a professional women's match

Ask:

- How quickly do players reposition after passing?
- How often do they scan before receiving?
- How fast does the ball move without becoming rushed?
- How often do they carry open space?
- How often do they switch fields intentionally?
- What do weak-side players do?
- What happens immediately after possession changes?
- How often are defenders forced to make decisions by movement or deception?
- Freeze the video at a random moment. Can you see evidence of coaching without hearing the coach?

For more on the freeze test, see [What Good Coaching Looks Like](good-coaching-signs.md).

Full professional women's matches (U.S., 2026–27):

- **NWSL:** TV and streaming partners, plus free games on NWSL+
- **WSL (England):** Paramount+

TV and streaming rights change often. Check the league's "How to Watch" page.

## Position study

When preparing for a role, watch one strong player in that position and ignore the ball whenever she does not have it.

Ask:

- Where does she stand when the ball is on the opposite side?
- How does she create an outlet?
- What does she do after passing?
- When does she step, drop, press, or delay?
- What does she do immediately when possession changes?

### One question for your position

When you review your own game, start with the question for your position. Your position card helps you picture these moments before the game; this question checks them afterward.

- [Goalkeeper (#1)](positions/goalkeeper.md): After I collected the ball, did I scan and restart possession quickly, or punt by habit?
- [Outside back (#2 / #3)](positions/outside-back.md): When we lost it, did I recover on an angle to where the attacker was going, or chase from behind?
- [Center back (#4 / #5)](positions/center-back.md): When nobody pressured me, did I carry or find midfield feet, or did I clear it?
- [Pivot (#6 / #8)](positions/defensive-midfield.md): Before I received, did I know where the pressure was, and was I at a different height from my partner?
- [Winger (#7 / #11)](positions/winger.md): When the ball was on the far side, was I preparing for the switch, or just watching?
- [Striker (#9)](positions/striker.md): Did my movement change what the center backs had to decide?
- [Attacking midfielder (#10)](positions/attacking-midfield.md): Did I find space between the lines, and did I move again after I passed?

## Sharing film safely

**Team film stays with the team.**

- Watch game film through the team's private link or app. Do not repost it publicly.
- Clips of a teammate are for learning. Describe, don't judge. Never share a teammate's mistake in a group chat, story, or comment.
- Questions about your film go to the coach through the team app, with a parent included.
- If a family asks for a clip of their daughter to come down, it comes down.
- No recording in locker rooms, restrooms, or changing areas. When watching other teams online, learn from them; don't repost or comment on their players.

## Final principle

Film review is not about proving that a player was right or wrong.

It is about building a better mental picture of the game.

**See more. Understand more. Choose better.**
