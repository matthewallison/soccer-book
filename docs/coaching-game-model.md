# Coaching Game Model & Development Manual

## 1. Purpose

The goal is to develop intelligent, technical, courageous players who can solve soccer problems themselves.

The team should be a vehicle for developing complete players, not merely a mechanism for winning the next match.

This document is for coaches. Players are **not** expected to consciously remember every item during games. The detailed principles here should be taught through representative training and reduced to simple cues for players.

The coaching system itself is a living system. We should be attached to good principles, not rigidly attached to one method.

Regularly ask:

- What is improving?
- What keeps appearing as a problem in games?
- Are practices transferring to matches?
- Are players understanding principles or merely following instructions?
- Has a player's development changed what role best suits her?
- Is one of our rules now limiting creativity rather than helping it?

## 2. Core mantras

### Practice
**SCAN. TALK. INTENTION. FASTER.**

Practice is where we deliberately raise the speed at which good soccer survives.

Faster means:

- scan sooner;
- communicate sooner;
- move sooner;
- pass more firmly;
- support more quickly;
- recover immediately;
- make decisions under greater pressure;
- reduce dead time and going through the motions.

It does not mean panic.

A useful practice question is:

**Are we actually playing faster, or are we just rushing?**

### Games
**CALM MIND. FAST SOCCER.**

The emotional message reverses on game day. Players trust the speed developed in training and avoid manufacturing speed through panic.

### Mistakes
**RESPOND. REFLECT. LEARN. NEXT PLAY.**

Mistakes from intelligent risk-taking are expected. Coaches should distinguish decision quality from execution quality and never create shame around brave, thoughtful attempts.

## 3. Competitive philosophy

**Winning is not the primary measure of development, but competing to win is part of development.**

Players should genuinely want to win and compete hard. Coaches maintain the longer-term perspective.

A team can lose while developing well and can win while developing poorly.

## 4. Base structure

The base structure can be viewed as a **4-2-3-1 that can behave like a 4-3-3** depending on which deeper midfielder advances and how the team occupies space.

The formation is a starting structure. The real objective is maintaining useful relationships.

### Midfield triangle

The 10 and two deeper midfielders should form a rotating triangle rather than three fixed points.

Useful principle:

**One shows. One protects. One threatens.**

The triangle should continually offer different heights and forward, sideways, and backward options.

## 5. The four moments

### When we have the ball

- Create width, depth, angles, and different heights.
- Progress when an advantage exists.
- Retain possession when progression is not advantageous.
- Carry when space is available.
- Manipulate defenders rather than merely pass around them.
- Prepare rest defense before possession is lost.

### When we lose the ball

Immediately decide:

- **Counterpress** if enough teammates are close and the ball can realistically be won quickly.
- **Recover** if the opponent escapes the first pressure.

### When they have the ball

- Protect central danger first.
- Stay compact.
- Pressure intelligently.
- Provide cover and balance.
- Delay counters when necessary.
- Anticipate the next transition.

### When we win the ball

- Scan forward immediately.
- Attack an advantage if one exists.
- If not, secure possession and build.

## 6. Possession with purpose

Possession is not an end in itself.

**Progress when we have an advantage. Keep the ball when we don't.**

Players should learn to look:

- diagonal;
- sideways;
- backward;
- forward.

A temporary training constraint such as **no direct vertical forward passes** can be useful to break the habit of automatic forward play. It should not become a permanent game rule.

Short is not automatically safer, and long is not automatically hopeful.

A 30-yard diagonal to a teammate's path can be a better possession action than three short passes into pressure.

We want **intentional long passing**, not blind clearances.

## 7. Possession at multiple scales

Rondos are valuable for:

- scanning;
- body shape;
- quick support;
- tight-space combination play;
- pressing cues;
- pressure resistance.

But rondos should not be the whole possession curriculum.

Large-space possession must also teach:

- carrying through open grass;
- switching the point of attack;
- longer ground and diagonal passing;
- weak-side awareness;
- line-breaking passes;
- exploiting breakaway opportunities;
- recognizing when the opponent has over-compressed around the ball.

A useful progression:

1. 4v2 or 5v2 rondo.
2. Medium-space possession with directional targets.
3. 7v7 or 8v8 with large spaces, wide channels, or switching incentives.
4. Full-field positional play.

**Find the biggest useful space.**

## 8. Carrying through space

Passing is not automatically superior to dribbling.

If no defender engages and open space exists, the player should often carry forward until someone must make a decision.

Early teaching cue:

**CARRY!**

The carry should trigger teammates to scan, move, create new angles, and recognize which defender is being fixed.

Over time, the verbal cue should be removed so the ball carrier recognizes it herself.

## 9. Fixing defenders

A central attacking concept:

**Make a defender choose.**

If a defender steps, someone else becomes freer. If a defender follows a run, space opens elsewhere. If a defender refuses to engage, keep carrying.

Players should progress from asking only "Who is open?" to asking "How can I make someone open?"

## 10. Deception

Predictable players are easy to defend.

Team cue:

**Look like you're changing your mind.**

Examples:

- look outside, pass inside;
- shape to pass, then carry;
- check toward the ball, then run behind;
- fake shot, then slip a pass;
- slow down, then accelerate;
- show one direction with body shape and escape another.

The goal is not tricks for their own sake. The goal is to manipulate the defender.

## 11. Third-player soccer

Sometimes the player receiving the first pass is not the real target.

**A → B → C**

Simple teaching phrase:

**Sometimes the player you pass to isn't the player you're trying to find.**

## 12. Pass and move

After passing, players should immediately ask:

**Where can I help next?**

They may:

- re-support;
- run beyond;
- pull a defender;
- create width;
- fill a vacated space;
- provide backward support;
- prepare for transition.

Team standard:

**NO HIDING. NO WATCHING. INFLUENCE THE NEXT ACTION.**

## 13. Everyone is an outlet

Every player is a potential passing option.

If you do not move to become an option, you are making the ball carrier's problem harder.

The player on the ball is not solely responsible for solving pressure. Teammates must help by creating angles, changing height, stretching space, and communicating.

## 14. Five-lane spacing

Players should understand the general five vertical channels:

**wide | half-space | center | half-space | wide**

The lanes are not cages. They are a visual tool for understanding spacing and avoiding unnecessary crowding.

## 15. Weak-side behavior

When the ball is on one side, the far side remains part of the play.

Weak-side players should consider:

- staying wide for the switch;
- moving into the half-space;
- attacking the far post;
- preparing for the next phase.

**Strong side attracts. Weak side prepares.**

## 16. Front-line rotations

The front three should rotate purposefully rather than run in three vertical lanes.

Principle:

**Exchange positions without losing attacking shape.**

Someone moves, someone fills, and someone preserves width or depth.

Rotations should be used to exploit different player profiles, create confusion, and get the right type of attacker into the right type of chance.

## 17. Fullback development

Outside backs should learn to:

- support possession;
- step into useful space;
- intercept proactively;
- carry when open;
- eventually recognize overlap, underlap, and hold/support choices.

If possession is lost after advancing, recovery effort is immediate and maximal.

### Pursuit angles

Recovering defenders should not simply chase the attacker from behind.

**Do not run to where the attacker is. Run to where she is going.**

Train angled recovery runs that cut off the dangerous path toward goal, then curve to influence the attacker away from danger.

This applies to outside backs, center backs, and any recovering defender.

## 18. Building from the back

Center backs should not treat receiving the ball as an emergency.

When one center back receives:

- the other opens as a switch option;
- midfield adjusts;
- outside backs support;
- the goalkeeper remains available.

The receiving CB should scan among multiple options:

- carry;
- play into midfield;
- switch;
- use an outside back;
- use the goalkeeper;
- recycle.

The horizontal CB pass should be available, not automatic.

## 19. Goalkeeper in possession

The goalkeeper is part of the possession structure.

Goalkeepers should become proficient at:

- receiving back passes;
- opening body shape;
- using both feet;
- playing short under reasonable pressure;
- helping switch play;
- recognizing where the press has created space.

After collecting the ball, the first thought should often be:

**Can we restart possession quickly and intelligently?**

Favor quick throws, rolls, and short/medium outlets when they are available. Long punts remain a tactical option, but should not be the automatic default.

**Quick possession before hopeful distance.**

## 20. Rest defense

Defending begins before possession is lost.

Players underneath the attack should already consider:

- opponent threats;
- likely turnover locations;
- whether an immediate counterpress would be possible;
- which central spaces must remain protected.

We do not want attacks that become five players forward followed by a desperate sprint race backward.

## 21. Delay defending

When the opponent has an advantage, the first defender may need to buy time rather than immediately win the ball.

Sequence:

**PROTECT → SLOW → SHOW → WAIT → WIN**

### Protect
Stay between the attacker and the most dangerous route to goal.

### Slow
Reduce the attacker's speed.

### Show
Influence her toward less dangerous space, usually away from the center unless the tactical picture says otherwise.

### Wait
Stay balanced. Do not dive in merely because the ball is nearby.

### Win
Attack the ball when the touch is poor, the attacker loses balance, support arrives, space disappears, or the challenge becomes favorable.

An excellent delay may contain no tackle.

## 22. Off-ball defensive positioning

Players should gather information about:

- ball;
- goal;
- teammates;
- opponents;
- dangerous space.

Useful shorthand:

**See the ball and the most dangerous information around you.**

Body orientation should allow a defender to influence passing lanes, close if the ball arrives, provide cover, and remain useful when possession changes.

**Defend from a position you can attack from.**

## 23. Compact defense and pressing

We defend collectively rather than as ten isolated marking assignments.

Priorities:

1. Protect central danger.
2. Stay connected vertically and horizontally.
3. Pressure when appropriate.
4. Provide cover and balance.
5. Shift together.

Useful pressing triggers include:

- poor first touch;
- backward pass;
- slow square pass;
- receiver facing her own goal;
- ball near the sideline;
- isolated receiver;
- local numerical advantage.

**We earn the right to press by staying compact enough to support the first defender.**

## 24. Individual 1v1 development

Every player should learn both attacking and defending.

Attacking:

- change direction;
- change speed;
- protect the ball;
- deceive;
- attack favorable 1v1s.

Defending:

- approach speed;
- body angle;
- delay;
- steering;
- tackling;
- recovery after being beaten.

No attacking player should be protected from defensive learning, and no defender should be denied on-ball development.

## 25. Risk and emotional safety

Creative soccer requires risk.

At U14, players will make mistakes while learning to receive under pressure, turn, carry, dribble, disguise passes, play through lines, combine, rotate, and make independent decisions.

A mistake should lead to useful reflection, never humiliation.

**Respond. Reflect. Learn. Next play.**

Teammates should support rather than blame, ridicule, roll eyes, or make a player afraid to try an intelligent action again.

## 26. Position rotation

Players should not become permanently defined by one position at this age.

In the most competitive matches, prioritize a strong team structure while still looking for reasonable opportunities to broaden roles.

Training, friendlies, scrimmages, and selected match situations should deliberately expand positional understanding.

**Compete with our best structure. Develop players beyond that structure.**

## 27. Advance role preparation

Whenever practical, tell each player her expected **primary role** about 48 hours before a match without announcing the starting lineup.

The player should review the relevant position card and mentally rehearse the four moments:

- when we have it;
- when we lose it;
- when they have it;
- when we win it.

If a player may cover two roles, provide a primary and secondary card to review.

Matchday instruction should mostly be reminders, not first-time teaching.

## 28. Individual development plans

Every player, starter or reserve, should regularly know:

- one current strength to build on;
- one current development priority;
- what improvement would look like.

These are not permanent labels and should be updated as players improve.

No player on the bench should be confused about what she needs to improve to earn greater opportunity.

## 29. Player ownership and peer learning

Players should help each other learn, but should not grade one another.

**Help each other learn. Don't grade each other.**

Encourage:

- questions;
- shared observations;
- demonstrations;
- useful information;
- specific praise.

Discourage:

- blame;
- bossiness;
- public criticism;
- acting as another player's authority.

Coaches should also periodically ask players what team problem they see and how they might solve it.

## 30. Team development culture

**Your effort is part of your teammate's training.**

Full-speed defending, transition effort, movement, communication, and honest competition create better repetitions for everyone.

Growth happens fastest when the team develops together.

## 31. Technical practice detail

### Passing

- firm when appropriate;
- usually on the ground;
- targeted to a specific foot or useful space;
- weighted to help the next action.

A pass should not merely arrive. It should help the receiver.

### Receiving

Train:

- scanning before arrival;
- open body shape;
- receiving across the body;
- first touch away from pressure;
- receiving into the next action;
- turning when possible;
- protecting when necessary.

A good first touch is not merely stopping the ball. It is using the arriving ball to begin the next action.

## 32. Maximum meaningful ball interaction

More meaningful ball interactions generally create more opportunities for development.

Warm-ups should minimize unnecessary lines, waiting, and dead balls.

Standard:

**BALL ALIVE. FEET ALIVE. BRAIN ALIVE.**

Static passing still has a place when a specific technical objective requires it, but ordinary warm-ups should keep players active and engaged.

## 33. Athletic development

Players should progressively develop:

- acceleration;
- deceleration;
- change of direction;
- coordination;
- balance;
- strength;
- mobility;
- repeat sprint ability;
- jumping/landing;
- ankle/lower-leg stiffness and elasticity.

Use age-appropriate plyometrics with emphasis on quality, landing control, and sensible workload.

Athletic work should support soccer, never be used as punishment.

## 34. Heading philosophy

**Head with purpose, not habit.**

High-value situations include:

- scoring;
- saving a goal;
- defending dangerous crosses;
- redirecting dangerous balls;
- situations where heading is clearly the best tactical action.

Players are not obligated to attack every high ball merely because it can be headed. Routine punts do not automatically require an aerial challenge.

Training exposure should remain age-appropriate and consistent with current governing-body guidance.

## 35. Film study

Players should learn to evaluate their own decisions rather than simply ask whether they played well.

Useful questions:

- What did I see?
- What options existed?
- What might happen 2–4 actions later?
- Was there a non-obvious or deceptive solution?
- Was the decision good?
- Was the execution good?

See the separate [Film Review Guide](film-review-guide.md).

## 36. Practice design should respond to games

A club curriculum can provide structure, but it should not replace coaching.

If matches reveal recurring problems, upcoming training should address them.

Examples:

- automatic vertical play;
- poor first touch under pressure;
- inability to switch;
- weak transition reactions;
- poor midfield spacing;
- inability to defend 1v1;
- lack of communication;
- players hiding after passing.

The recurring coaching question should be:

**What problem from our games are we teaching them to solve?**

## 37. Game-based learning and constraints

Training should contain realistic teammates, opponents, direction, space, pressure, transition, and consequences whenever practical.

Constraints can temporarily emphasize a principle:

- no forward passes;
- touch limits;
- required switches;
- bonus points for carries;
- goals after third-player combinations;
- scoring after changing fields.

But constraints should eventually disappear.

The goal is not players who obey artificial rules. The goal is players who recognize the underlying soccer problem independently.

## 38. Create advantages

Teach players to recognize and create:

- **numerical advantage** — 2v1, 3v2;
- **positional advantage** — receiving between lines;
- **spatial advantage** — open grass;
- **qualitative advantage** — isolating a player in a matchup she can exploit.

Ask:

**Where is our advantage?**

If none exists:

**How can we create one?**

## 39. Coach communication

A core coaching question:

**What did you see?**

This encourages players to explain perception and intention rather than simply defend themselves after a mistake.

The best coaching should progressively make the coach less necessary.

## 40. Team identity

We want a team recognized for players who:

- scan constantly;
- communicate;
- play with intention;
- train at speed;
- remain calm in games;
- demand and support the ball;
- use deception;
- move after passing;
- carry open space;
- switch play;
- exploit weak-side space;
- understand third-player combinations;
- defend collectively;
- delay rather than dive in;
- use good pursuit angles;
- react immediately in transition;
- support teammates;
- take ownership of their development.

Above everything:

**NO HIDING. NO WATCHING. THINK, MOVE, INFLUENCE THE GAME.**
