# Coaching Game Model & Development Manual

## 1. Purpose

The goal is to develop intelligent, technical, courageous players who can solve soccer problems themselves.

The team should be a vehicle for developing complete players, not merely a mechanism for winning the next match.

We are a committed, technical team without dominant athletes. We will not win a constant sprint race.

**We win through spacing, information, combinations, possession, defensive organization, and collective decisions.**

This document is for coaches. Players are **not** expected to consciously remember every item during games. The detailed principles here should be taught through representative training and reduced to simple cues for players.

### Where to start

Do not try to install 45 sections at once. Build in layers, and move on when the previous layer shows up in games without reminders:

1. **Culture and the four moments** (§2–5, §27–33): mantras, mistake culture, courage, and the habit of reacting the instant the ball changes hands.
2. **Spacing and outlets** (§12–15, §18–19): everyone becomes an option, pass and move, five lanes, building from the back with the goalkeeper.
3. **The middle gear** (§6–8): recognizing when pressure is escaped and the field is big, carrying, switching, purposeful longer passing.
4. **Defensive coordination** (§20–26): rest defense, delay, marking distance, pressing together, restarts, game state.
5. **Rotations and advanced detail** (§9–11, §16–17): fixing defenders, deception, third-player runs, front-line and outside-back movement.

Practice design (§40–41) and the development system (§29–31) run throughout.

The coaching system itself is a living system. We should be attached to good principles, not rigidly attached to one method.

Regularly ask:

- What is improving?
- What keeps appearing as a problem in games?
- Are practices transferring to matches?
- Are players understanding principles or merely following instructions?
- Has a player's development changed what role best suits her?
- Is one of our rules now limiting creativity rather than helping it?
- Is this environment making each player better, or only helping us win this weekend?
- Could someone freeze our game at a random moment and see evidence of coaching?

For the signs to look for, see [What Good Coaching Looks Like](good-coaching-signs.md).

## 2. Core mantras

Four mantras carry the whole model. Players know them by memory; the rest of this manual explains what they mean on the field.

**Say it, then use it.** Players say the practice mantra together, out loud, as the first thing at every practice, and the game mantra as the first thing in every game warm-up. It takes under 30 seconds. Coaches use the same words as live cues and in feedback. Now and then, ask a player for a quick example of one word from the last game.

### Practice
**SCAN. TALK. INTENTION. FASTER.**

Practice is where we deliberately raise the speed at which good soccer survives.

Faster means:

- scan sooner
- communicate sooner
- move sooner
- pass more firmly
- support more quickly
- recover immediately
- make decisions under greater pressure
- reduce dead time and going through the motions

It does not mean panic.

A useful practice question is: **Are we actually playing faster, or are we just rushing?**

### Games
**CALM MIND. FAST SOCCER.**

The emotional message reverses on game day. Players trust the speed developed in training and avoid manufacturing speed through panic.

When first teaching the game mantra, use the full version: "Calm mind. Fast soccer. See it before you get it." Once the idea sticks, the first four words are enough.

Calm means clear attention, not low energy. Fast soccer is not getting rid of the ball quickly: it can mean holding the ball an extra second because she already scanned, playing backward, or carrying 15 yards because nobody stepped. Never tell a player to "calm down" before a pressure moment (see §28 for better language).

### After mistakes
**RESPOND. REFLECT. LEARN. NEXT PLAY.**

Mistakes from intelligent risk-taking are expected. Coaches should distinguish decision quality from execution quality and never create shame around brave, thoughtful attempts.

Respond and next play take seconds during play. Reflect and learn happen at breaks, after games, and on film.

### Pressure moments
**WANT THE MOMENT.**

Nerves are normal. They are the body getting ready. The goal is not to eliminate fear. The goal is to stop fear from making the decision (§28).

## 3. Competitive philosophy

**Winning is not the primary measure of development, but competing to win is part of development.**

Players should genuinely want to win and compete hard.

Wanting to win is developmental. It creates:

- urgency
- concentration
- resilience
- defensive effort and recovery runs
- communication
- emotional investment

Each group owns a different part:

- **Coach:** long-term perspective
- **Players:** competitive urgency
- **Team:** brave enough to develop while still fighting to win

A team can lose while developing well and can win while developing poorly.

Keep the scoreboard in proportion:

- Before games, talk about what we will do (process goals), not the score.
- Play to win the next action, not to avoid losing.
- No player carries a result alone. We review losses as team problems to solve and never blame them on one player.

Players hear it as: **Want to win. Play for the next action, not the scoreboard.**

Be clear about how roles and playing time are earned. Players who stay in the game more often describe coaches who take both winning and enjoyment seriously, set individual goals, and base playing time on effort and skill.

## 4. Base structure

The base structure can be viewed as a **4-2-3-1 that can behave like a 4-3-3** depending on which deeper midfielder advances and how the team occupies space.

The formation is a starting structure. The real objective is maintaining useful relationships.

Two deeper midfielders are a developmental bridge: they give security while the team learns to possess, recover, and position. A single pivot can come later.

### Role key

Role numbers used in this handbook (the right side takes the lower number):

- goalkeeper (#1)
- outside backs (#2 right, #3 left)
- center backs (#4 / #5)
- pivots, the two deeper midfielders (#6 / #8)
- wingers (#7 right, #11 left)
- striker, the 9 (#9)
- attacking midfielder, the 10 (#10)

### Midfield triangle

The 10 and the two pivots form a rotating triangle, not three fixed dots.

**One shows. One protects. One threatens.**

Who does each job can change:

- If one pivot drops, another player can rise.
- If one pivot advances, another protects underneath.
- If the 10 moves or drops, the triangle adjusts.

The 10 is free to move the triangle, and the pivots adjust to keep its shape. Preserve the relationships, not the coordinates.

The triangle should continually offer different heights and forward, sideways, and backward options.

The pivots help control tempo. Not every touch must accelerate the attack. Sometimes the best action is forward, sometimes a switch or a carry, and sometimes it is securing possession and letting the team reorganize.

### Without the ball

Without the ball we usually defend as a 4-4-2 or 4-4-1-1: the 10 either steps up beside the 9 or screens their deepest midfielder, the wingers drop level with the pivots, and the far-side winger narrows. Which option the 10 uses is a game-plan choice.

## 5. The four moments

### When we have it

- Create width, depth, angles, and different heights.
- Progress when an advantage exists.
- Retain possession when progression is not advantageous.
- Carry when space is available.
- Recognize whether the problem is small-space or large-space (§7).
- Manipulate defenders rather than merely pass around them.
- Prepare rest defense before possession is lost.

### When we lose it

Immediately decide:

- **Counterpress** if enough teammates are close and the ball can realistically be won quickly.
- **Recover** if the opponent escapes the first pressure.

**First player: the ball. Next players: the nearest passes. Everyone else: compact.**

- Counterpress as a short, all-out burst of a few seconds.
- If the ball escapes, stop chasing: delay, protect the middle, and recover into shape.

### When they have it

- Protect central danger first.
- Stay compact.
- Pressure intelligently.
- Provide cover and balance.
- Delay counters when necessary.
- Anticipate the next transition.

### When we win it

- Scan forward immediately.
- Attack an advantage if one exists.
- If not, secure possession and build.

Each position card turns these four moments into role-specific pictures (§30).

## 6. Possession with purpose

Possession is not an end in itself.

**Progress when we have an advantage. Keep the ball when we don't.**

We don't play forward because we're impatient. We play forward because we've created an advantage.

If the advantage is not there: calm mind, keep it, move them, create one. The best forward pass is not always the earliest forward pass.

Players should learn to look:

- diagonal
- sideways
- backward
- forward

A temporary training constraint such as **no direct vertical forward passes** can be useful to break the habit of automatic forward play. It should not become a permanent game rule. Keep bans like this short, because a long ban removes a picture players must learn to read (§41).

Short is not automatically safer, and long is not automatically hopeful. See §7 for when a longer pass is the better possession action.

## 7. Possession at multiple scales — the middle gear

Rondos are valuable for teaching:

- scanning
- body shape
- quick support
- tight-space combination play
- pressing cues
- pressure resistance
- playing through immediate pressure

But rondos teach only one scale of the game, and they should not be the whole possession curriculum.

A major developmental problem occurs when players learn how to survive in a small space but never learn what to do **after they escape it**.

The result can become: **short → short → short → short → trapped → panic clearance**.

Players keep solving the same small-space problem even after a larger-space solution has become available. The team is left with two gears: tight-space short passing and panic clearance.

We need another gear.

### The middle gear

The middle gear begins when the team escapes immediate pressure and the field opens. It is large-space possession: carrying, switching, longer intentional passing, weak-side recognition, and exploiting breakaways.

Now players must recognize:

- open grass
- an isolated defender
- a weak side
- a long diagonal lane
- a breakaway opportunity
- an unopposed carrying lane
- space behind the next defensive line
- an opponent that has over-compressed around the ball

At that moment, possession should expand.

The player should ask:

**Has the problem changed?**

If the pressure is gone, the solution should often change too.

Possible actions include:

- carry 10, 20, or 30 yards into open space
- switch the point of attack
- hit a purposeful 20–40 yard ground or diagonal pass
- break a defensive line
- find the weak side
- attack an isolated defender
- release a runner into space
- accelerate the attack before the defense can recover

Team cue:

**SMALL SPACE: COMBINE. BIG SPACE: EXPLOIT.**

Possession should expand and contract with the available space. When the field is tight, combine. When the field opens, use it.

We do not want players escaping one crowded area only to recreate another crowded area five yards away.

The purpose of combination play is often to **escape pressure and reach better space**.

### Short does not mean possession, and long does not mean direct

A short pass into pressure is not automatically safer or more intelligent than a longer pass into space.

A 30-yard diagonal to a teammate in stride may preserve possession far better than three five-yard passes into a collapsing defense.

Likewise, a longer pass is not automatically "kick and run." The difference is intention. We want intentional long passing, not blind clearances.

**Purposeful distance, not hopeful distance.**

Players should learn the difference between:

- clearing the ball because they are panicking
- deliberately using space with a longer pass

### The goal of escaping pressure

When we successfully combine through pressure, the reward should often be **more space**. Players must learn to recognize that moment.

Useful coaching questions:

- Did we escape the pressure?
- Is the field bigger now?
- Where is the biggest useful space?
- Can someone carry?
- Is the weak side available?
- Can we attack before they recover?
- Do we need another short pass, or has the problem changed?

The team should not remain psychologically trapped in the rondo after physically escaping it.

**Find the biggest useful space.**

### Training the middle gear

Training should deliberately expose players to changing field sizes and changing solutions.

A useful progression:

1. **Small-space rondo** (such as 4v2 or 5v2): survive pressure, scan, support, and combine quickly.
2. **Escape rondo:** after a set number of passes or a successful split, the ball exits into a much larger area. Players must immediately recognize that the problem has changed. Reward carrying, switching, or attacking space.
3. **Medium directional possession:** use targets, end zones, gates, or neutral players. Teach players to escape one pressure area and find the next useful space.
4. **Large-space 7v7 or 8v8:** intentionally create large weak-side spaces with wide channels or switching incentives. Reward switches, carries, line-breaking passes, and purposeful longer balls.
5. **Full-field positional play:** allow the complete spectrum, from tight combination → escape → expansion → attack → recycle if needed.

Training should not repeatedly constrain players to areas where every useful action is 5–15 yards.

Players need regular experience seeing and executing:

- 20-yard carries
- 25–40 yard diagonal passes
- large switches
- weak-side runs
- breakaway decisions
- attacks against retreating defenders

Choose the space on purpose:

- Small spaces give more touches, more pressure, and faster decisions.
- Big spaces give longer passes, more running, and more carrying and switching.
- Rondos have no goal to attack. Keep them short and sharp, then add direction, targets, or goals.
- Big-space games need real room, in the part of the field where the game problem happens. As a rough guide, a 7v7 plus keepers needs close to half a field, not a penalty box.

A practice curriculum that teaches only small-space possession will produce players who are comfortable only when the game is small.

We want players who can recognize **when to make the game small and when to make it big.**

## 8. Carrying through space

Passing is not automatically superior to dribbling.

If no defender engages and open space exists, the player should often carry forward until someone must make a decision.

Early teaching cue, called by the whole team: **Carry!**

When everyone calls it, everyone is learning to recognize open space, not just the ball carrier.

The carry should trigger teammates to scan, move, create new angles, and recognize which defender is being fixed.

Over time, the team's Carry! call should fade so the ball carrier recognizes open space herself.

Two related big-space cues work the same way: **Expand** when the team has escaped pressure, and **Go** when an immediate transition or breakaway exists (see [Communication & Movement Fundamentals](communication-and-movement.md#big-space-cues)). Anyone can call them; the player on the ball still owns the decision.

## 9. Fixing defenders

A central attacking concept:

**Make a defender choose.**

If a defender steps, someone else becomes freer. If a defender follows a run, space opens elsewhere. If a defender refuses to engage, keep carrying.

Players should progress from asking only "Who is open?" to asking "How can I make someone open?"

## 10. Deception

Predictable players are easy to defend.

Team cue:

**Look like you're changing your mind.**

Examples:

- look outside, pass inside
- shape to pass, then carry
- check toward the ball, then run behind
- fake shot, then slip a pass
- slow down, then accelerate
- show one direction with body shape and escape another

The goal is not tricks for their own sake. The goal is to manipulate the defender.

## 11. Third-player soccer

Sometimes the player receiving the first pass is not the real target. The pattern is **A → B → C**.

Simple teaching phrase:

**Sometimes the player you pass to isn't the player you're trying to find.**

Timing matters: the third player moves the moment a defender jumps to the first receiver, into the space that defender just left.

## 12. Pass and move

**Move on the pass. Move again after you pass.** After passing, players should immediately ask: **Where can I help next?**

They may:

- re-support
- run beyond
- pull a defender
- create width
- fill a vacated space
- provide backward support
- prepare for transition

A player who has just passed is still responsible for influencing the next action.

Team standard:

**No hiding. No watching. Influence the game.**

## 13. Everyone is an outlet

Every player is a potential passing option.

If you do not move to become an option, you are making the ball carrier's problem harder.

The player on the ball is not solely responsible for solving pressure. Teammates must help by creating angles, changing height, stretching space, and communicating.

At every moment, players ask: **Where could I go right now that would make me useful?**

## 14. Five-lane spacing

Players should understand the general five vertical channels: wide, half-space, center, half-space, and wide.

The lanes are not cages. They are a visual tool for understanding spacing and avoiding unnecessary crowding.

**Try to occupy all five lanes, with no two teammates in the same lane at the same height.**

## 15. Weak-side behavior

When the ball is on one side, the far side remains part of the play.

Weak-side players should consider:

- staying wide for the switch
- moving into the half-space
- attacking the far post
- preparing for the next phase

**Strong side attracts. Weak side prepares.**

Without the ball, the far side has a different job: the strong side pressures, and the weak side tucks in.

The far-side winger narrows to keep midfield compact (§4), and the far outside back tucks toward the center backs (§17).

## 16. Front-line rotations

The front three should rotate purposefully rather than run in three vertical lanes.

Principle: **Exchange positions without losing attacking shape.**

Someone moves, someone fills, and someone preserves width or depth.

Rotations should be used to exploit different player profiles, create confusion, and get the right type of attacker into the right type of chance.

Every rotation needs a reason:

- drag a defender out of position
- create confusion about who marks whom
- create a central runner
- open a channel
- disguise who will receive

Match profile to phase:

- **Settled attack:** A target or finishing striker pins the center backs, attacks crosses and cutbacks, and gives an aerial target.
- **Open game or transition:** The fastest, most direct attacker carries into space, attacks isolated defenders, runs beyond, and finishes the break herself.

This uses current strengths inside a game. Long-term roles are not assigned by size or speed (§29).

Examples:

- the 9 checks toward the ball while the fastest winger runs beyond
- a winger carries inside while the 9 stays central as the finishing target
- wide and central attackers cross their runs in transition to get the fastest runner central

### The 9 and the 10

**One comes. One goes.**

The 9 and the 10 should not automatically occupy the same height.

- If the 9 checks toward midfield, the 10 can run beyond and temporarily become the highest central attacker.
- If the 10 checks toward the ball, the 9 stays high and pins the center backs.
- They may keep cycling vertically during an attack, and the 9 can reoccupy the high line later.

Positions set the structure; movement changes who occupies the spaces. The 9 does not have to be the highest central player every second.

### Diagonal runs

Wingers should not picture only running straight down the touchline. Teach them to:

- start wide, then run diagonally behind
- attack inside when the 9 checks
- exchange lanes with the 9 when useful
- become the temporary central runner in transition
- make crossing runs that force defenders to decide who follows
- arrive at the far post from the weak side

Wingers may occasionally switch sides or cross attacking lanes when doing so creates an advantage without losing team width.

The 9 makes diagonal runs, not only straight ones, and moves center backs intentionally. She lets another attacker become the central runner when that creates the better advantage.

## 17. Outside back development

Outside backs (sometimes called fullbacks) should learn to:

- support possession
- step into useful space
- intercept proactively
- carry when open
- eventually recognize overlap, underlap, and hold/support choices

If possession is lost after advancing, recovery effort is immediate and maximal. The mentality is proactive going forward and relentless recovering: advancing is not a mistake if it is intelligent and the response after possession loss is immediate.

Outside backs can step and intercept because the players behind them know how to delay. Train both together.

**Outside backs step. Center backs and pivots delay until they recover.**

### Outside back and winger

The outside back and her winger should never blindly occupy the same lane.

- If the winger comes inside, the outside back may provide width.
- If the winger stays wide, the outside back supports underneath or recognizes the underlap or inside support.

### Weak-side defending

When the ball is on the opposite side, the far outside back:

- tucks toward central danger
- sees the far-post runner
- stays connected to the center back
- is ready to expand again when possession changes

Both outside backs high at the same time only works when the pivots stay underneath the ball (§20).

### Pursuit angles

Recovering defenders should not simply chase the attacker from behind.

**Do not run to where the attacker is. Run to where she is going.**

Train angled recovery runs that cut off the dangerous path toward goal, then curve to influence the attacker away from danger.

This applies to outside backs, center backs, and any recovering defender. The player-facing version is in [Communication & Movement Fundamentals](communication-and-movement.md#pursuit-angles).

## 18. Building from the back

Center backs should not treat receiving the ball as an emergency. Calm is a weapon.

**Safe does not automatically mean clearance.**

If time and space exist, solve the soccer problem.

When one center back receives:

- the other opens as a switch option
- midfield adjusts
- outside backs support
- the goalkeeper remains available

The receiving center back should scan among multiple options:

- carry
- play into midfield
- switch
- use an outside back
- use the goalkeeper
- recycle

The horizontal pass between center backs should be available, not automatic. A sideways carry can change the passing angle when forward is closed, and after passing into midfield she steps up to support the next action.

### Goal kicks and pressure

- Center backs may receive inside our penalty area. Opponents must stay outside it until the ball is kicked.
- Set up quickly. Under the 2026/27 Laws of the Game, a delayed goal kick can be counted down and become their corner.
- If they press high with everyone, the space is behind them. A driven pass to a runner is intentional possession, not a panic clearance.
- If they drop off to stop it, play short and build.

**Short invites the press. Long punishes it. Choose on purpose.**

## 19. Goalkeeper in possession

This handbook is not a goalkeeper training curriculum. Shot-stopping, handling, and positioning belong to goalkeeper-specific training. This section sets her role in team possession.

The goalkeeper is part of the possession structure: the eleventh possession player, available behind the center backs. Back passes are normal soccer, not emergencies.

Goalkeepers should become proficient at:

- receiving back passes
- opening body shape
- using both feet
- playing short under reasonable pressure
- helping switch play
- recognizing where the press has created space

**If you decide to come, come. If you decide to play, play.**

She does not need to make every save or every pass perfect. She makes the next decision completely, communicates early (before danger develops), organizes the players in front of her, and recovers emotionally right away after a mistake or a goal.

After collecting the ball, the first thought should often be: **Can we restart possession quickly and intelligently?**

Favor quick throws, rolls, and short/medium outlets when they are available. Long punts remain a tactical option, but should not be the automatic default. Under the Laws of the Game, a goalkeeper who holds the ball in her hands for more than 8 seconds concedes a corner kick, with the referee counting down the last 5 seconds.

**Quick possession before hopeful distance.**

## 20. Rest defense

Defending begins before possession is lost.

Players underneath the attack should already consider:

- opponent threats
- likely turnover locations
- whether an immediate counterpress would be possible
- which central spaces must remain protected

**Rest defense rule of thumb: one more than the attackers they leave up.**

- If they leave two forwards up, keep three behind the ball: usually both center backs plus a pivot or a tucked-in outside back.
- The next line (usually the pivots) stays close enough to counterpress the first pass forward.
- Both outside backs high at once? The pivots stay underneath the ball.

This is a rule of thumb from the professional game, not a fixed law.

We do not want attacks that become five players forward followed by a desperate sprint race backward.

## 21. Delay defending

When the opponent has an advantage, the first defender may need to buy time rather than immediately win the ball.

Sequence:

**Protect → slow → show → wait → win**

- **Protect:** Stay between the attacker and the most dangerous route to goal.
- **Slow:** Reduce the attacker's speed.
- **Show:** Influence her toward less dangerous space, usually away from the center unless the tactical picture says otherwise.
- **Wait:** Stay balanced. Do not dive in merely because the ball is nearby.
- **Win:** Attack the ball when the touch is poor, the attacker loses balance, support arrives, space disappears, or the challenge becomes favorable.

An excellent delay may contain no tackle. For approach and body shape, see [Communication & Movement Fundamentals](communication-and-movement.md).

## 22. Off-ball defensive positioning

Useful shorthand: **See the ball and the most dangerous space or opponent.** The player-facing version is in [Communication & Movement Fundamentals](communication-and-movement.md#defensive-vision).

Do not let a defender become so focused on one mark that she loses the most dangerous information around her.

Body orientation should allow a defender to influence passing lanes, close if the ball arrives, provide cover, and remain useful when possession changes.

**Defend from a position you can attack from.**

### How tight to mark

**Protect dangerous space first. Get tighter as the ball and danger get closer.**

Get compact first. Then pick up the dangerous opponent in your area.

How close to stand depends on:

- where the ball is
- how dangerous the opponent's position is
- whether she can receive the next pass
- which way she is facing
- whether the passer is under pressure
- what cover is behind the defender

Get tight on dead balls (free kicks, corners, and throw-ins), on crosses, and when stepping to win the ball.

### Protecting the box

- Ball wide in our third: the pivots protect the top of the box and the cutback lane.
- Crosses: center backs stay goal-side, see ball and runner, and attack the ball in front of them.
- Ball on the far side: the far-side winger tucks inside to keep midfield compact.

## 23. Compact defense and pressing

We defend collectively rather than as ten isolated marking assignments.

Priorities:

1. Protect central danger.
2. Stay connected vertically and horizontally.
3. Pressure when appropriate.
4. Provide cover and balance.
5. Shift together.

Pressing together:

- **When one presses, the nearest teammates go with her.** They close the nearest passes, and the line behind moves up.
- **Lock them on one side.** Curve the first run to cut off the switch, and use the touchline as an extra defender.
- **Ball pressured: the back line steps up.** Ball unpressured and the passer can look up: the back line drops.

Useful pressing triggers include:

- poor first touch
- backward pass
- slow square pass
- long square pass across their back line
- receiver facing her own goal
- their goalkeeper receiving under pressure
- ball near the sideline
- isolated receiver
- local numerical advantage

**We earn the right to press by staying compact enough to support the first defender.**

The nearest defender does not always tackle. Who steps depends on angle, cover, and triggers. Sometimes the nearest player delays while a better-positioned teammate wins the ball.

### Center-back partnership

**One steps, one covers.**

The two center backs never both attack the same problem and never both retreat from it. When one steps to win the ball, the other tucks in behind her and covers the space she left. The pivots follow the same idea: never both leave the center.

### Against direct or physical teams

**Win the second ball. Then play on the ground.**

These are coaching practices for a team without dominant athletes:

- Pressure the passer so long balls are hurried. If she has time to look up, the back line drops before the kick (the step/drop rule above).
- One player contests the first ball, the nearest teammate covers behind for the flick-on, and the pivots surround the drop zone to win the second ball.
- Keep the gap between midfield and the back line small.
- The goalkeeper starts high enough to sweep balls over the top.
- Arrive early and win body position. Not every high ball needs a header (§37).

## 24. Restarts and set pieces

**Restarts are free chances. Organize them quickly; don't improvise them.**

As an elite benchmark, not a youth norm, set plays produced just over a quarter of the goals in the 2021/22 Women's Super League in England. Several restart timing rules (goal kicks, throw-ins, the goalkeeper's 8 seconds) are recent Laws of the Game changes; check league adoption each season.

Our corners and wide free kicks:

- attack the front post, the goalmouth, and the far post: arrive, don't camp
- one player at the top of the box for second balls
- enough players home to stop the counter (§20)

Their corners and wide free kicks:

- a few players own the front post and six-yard box while others mark the most dangerous attackers
- one player takes away the short corner
- win the first contact, clear high and wide, then step out together

Other restarts:

- free kicks: quick if an advantage exists, organized if not
- kickoffs: one rehearsed first action that keeps the ball
- goal kicks: read the press (§18)

Throw-ins should be **quick and usually short**:

- two options: one checks short, one threatens long
- sideways or backward throws, which keep the ball more often than long throws down the line into a crowd (in elite men's data)
- the thrower steps back on as the return pass
- no stalling: under the 2026/27 Laws of the Game, a delayed throw-in can be given to the opponent

Keep routines few and simple. Restart jobs are assigned before the match on the [Weekend Role Sheet](templates/weekend-role-sheet.md) (§30). For U13 players, set-piece rehearsals count toward heading limits (§37).

## 25. Game state

**Winning: keep it, don't just kick it.**

- keep the ball and play more of the game in their half
- lower the risk in our own third, not everywhere
- no cheap fouls or corners near our box

**Losing: faster, not frantic.**

- quick restarts and one extra runner in the box
- rest defense kept (§20) so one counter does not end the game

We never waste time dishonestly. Game management is a skill, not gamesmanship, and under the 2026/27 Laws of the Game, restart countdowns make stalling costly anyway.

## 26. Individual 1v1 development

Every player should learn both attacking and defending.

Attacking:

- change direction
- change speed
- protect the ball
- deceive
- attack favorable 1v1s

Defending:

- approach speed
- body angle
- delay
- steering
- tackling
- recovery after being beaten
- managing offside: holding, stepping, and dropping together as a line

No attacking player should be protected from defensive learning, and no defender should be denied on-ball development.

Offside awareness belongs to every defender and midfielder, not only center backs.

## 27. Risk and emotional safety

Creative soccer requires risk.

At U14, players will make mistakes while learning to receive under pressure, turn, carry, dribble, disguise passes, play through lines, combine, rotate, and make independent decisions.

A mistake should lead to useful reflection, never humiliation.

**RESPOND. REFLECT. LEARN. NEXT PLAY.**

Teammates should support rather than blame, ridicule, roll eyes, or make a player afraid to try an intelligent action again. The player-facing version is [Mistakes are learning moments](team-culture-and-leadership.md#mistakes-are-learning-moments).

Why this matters: in national survey data, girls' sport participation starts to drop around ages 12–13. Girls who quit report more worry about messing up and losing and more self-consciousness than girls who stay, and young athletes who quit report more teasing. A brave, supportive team keeps players in the game long enough to develop.

**Team reset:** the players choose one shared gesture (for example, brushing the shoulder). After a mistake, the player uses it herself, and teammates and coaches add "Next play." Practice it in training so it is automatic in games.

### Risk is contextual

The same dribble can be brave in the attacking third and careless as the last defender with no cover. Read:

- field location
- pressure on the ball
- cover behind the play
- the game situation

A careless decision ignores that picture. An intelligent risk reads it and still sometimes fails.

**We will correct careless decisions, but we will not punish intelligent ideas simply because they did not work.**

### What coaches avoid

Coaches avoid:

- subbing a player off as a reaction to a brave mistake
- sarcasm, yelling about errors, or comparing players publicly
- punishment running (§36)

**Praise brave decisions out loud.** Correct details specifically, away from the group when possible and always in view of others.

Revisit this list mid-season. Coaching habits slip back without reminders.

## 28. Courage and responsibility

**WANT THE MOMENT.**

Youth soccer gives players chances to practice courage before the stakes are truly high. A penalty kick, difficult pass, 1v1 duel, late-game shot, or moment of leadership can feel enormous, but at U14 these are primarily development opportunities.

At U14, the real loss is not missing the penalty. The real loss is avoiding the chance to learn how you respond under pressure.

**We should fear wasting developmental opportunities more than we fear temporary failure.**

We want players who think: "This is hard. Good. I want the chance to learn from it."

Avoiding pressure is a normal human response, not a character flaw. Even professionals rush when a miss feels costly, and rushed kicks miss more. The more a kick seems to matter, the harder it gets, so keep each kick in proportion: one kick, one lesson. The goal is not to eliminate fear. The goal is to stop fear from making the decision.

Wanting the moment never means playing through pain or hiding an injury. Coaches never praise a hidden injury as toughness; they thank a player for speaking up. See [Injuries, Recovery, and Return to Play](injuries-and-return-to-play.md).

The player-facing version, with the routine for any pressure moment (Breathe → decide → commit → execute) and the penalty routine (Pick. Place. Breathe. Strike.), is in [Team Culture: Want the moment](team-culture-and-leadership.md#want-the-moment).

### Developing hesitant players

Treat reluctance like a weak foot: develop it privately and progressively, never expose it. First ask her privately why she hesitates (trust in her strike, a recent miss, an injury, something social).

- **Validate, then expect.** "Nervous is normal. That's your body getting ready. You're ready for this step."
- **Change the language.** Instead of "Don't be nervous" or "Calm down," say "That's your body getting ready. Do your routine." Instead of "What if I miss?" ask "Where's your spot?"
- **Talk skill, not luck.** Never call shootouts a lottery. Separately from the finisher, give target reps to all four corners.
- **One stressor at a time.** Add the next one when she manages the current one.
- **Praise the step forward and the routine**, not only the goal.

Hesitant player ladder, moving up when she is ready (with a nudge): no keeper → keeper → small group watching → full team → named order in a scrimmage shootout → match.

Being watched is a big stressor at this age. Build audience size gradually. Never single out a player in front of the group for declining; follow up one-to-one, in view of others (§44). Support high-expectation players too: the top scorer, captain, or usual taker feels extra pressure.

### Practice finisher

Most sessions: one kick each, walking from halfway (about 5 minutes). Occasionally: a full shootout with a set order and sudden death.

1. Walk from halfway. Pick the spot before the walk.
2. Place, step back, whistle, one breath, strike.
3. Goal: celebrate together. Miss: teammates go to her.

Small-group versions are fine for players still climbing the ladder. Change one condition at a time: silent watching or normal noise, telling the keeper your side, the coach announcing the order. Rotate keepers; keepers kick too.

Keep stakes shared and light. Consequences belong to the whole group and stay playful, such as a team target for a small shared reward. Sometimes the target is the routine (spot picked, no rushing), not goals. No running for misses. No public miss counts. Noise, never personal comments. Nobody is "the one who cost us."

Debrief the process: **Did you pick a spot? Take your time? Commit?**

### Shootout order

Everyone trains to be ready; the coach sets the order. Ask during the week who wants a kick, never at the whistle. Before a tournament, share a first five with backups. In lower-stakes games, rotate the order so more players get real kicks. Own the outcome. Only players on the field at the final whistle may kick, and every eligible player kicks before anyone kicks twice (IFAB Laws of the Game; check tournament and league rules each season).

Families never critique a missed penalty or a player who declines one ([Parent Partnership](parent-partnership.md#on-the-sideline)). An example development priority built on this section is in §31.

## 29. Position rotation

Players should not become permanently defined by one position at this age.

In the most competitive matches, prioritize a strong team structure while still looking for reasonable opportunities to broaden roles.

Training, friendlies, scrimmages, and selected match situations should deliberately expand positional understanding.

**Compete with our best structure. Develop players beyond that structure.**

Coach the player she can be at 15–16, not only this season's position.

**A current position should be one position she can play, not the only position she knows how to play.**

Make rotation real, not occasional:

- Over a season, every player gets meaningful time in at least one role outside her usual line, in training and in selected games.
- Tell her what the new role is meant to teach her.
- Develop at least two players for every key role, especially center back and the pivots. If only one player can do a job, the team has a development problem.
- When a player asks to rotate, answer with a path: some minutes in another role, rotation in training, or a teammate learning her role while she learns another.

### Reliability, size, and confidence

Reliability should not lock a player into one role. A dependable player in a hard position is easy to leave there. That helps the team today and puts the cost on her.

Do not assign roles by current height, speed, or physical maturity. Players mature at different rates, and early size can hide or exaggerate future ability. Center back is the role most often handed to whoever is biggest now, and future coaches may choose positions by different physical profiles (§36).

Players in defensive roles can start to fear the ball because mistakes near our goal feel expensive. Protect their on-ball confidence. The goal is a soccer player who can defend, not a defender. For the player-facing version, see [Team role and development role](team-culture-and-leadership.md#team-role-and-development-role).

## 30. Advance role preparation

**Preparation happens before the game. Thinking happens during the game.**

Whenever practical, tell each player her expected **primary role** about 48 hours before a match without announcing the starting lineup. Share roles in person at practice or through the team app with parents included, never as a private message to one player. Roles may still change before or during the match: the notice is for preparation, not a promise of a position or minutes.

Plan on the [Weekend Role Sheet](templates/weekend-role-sheet.md). The coach keeps the full table (primary role, secondary role, restart jobs, one focus), and each player receives only her own row. If a player may cover two roles, provide a primary and secondary card to review.

### Position cards

Each [position card](positions/index.md) is a pregame preparation and visualization tool, not a miniature version of this manual. It gives the player:

- her purpose in the role
- a role-specific pregame mindset
- moments to picture
- the four moments in brief
- her most important relationships
- a fallback for when she is unsure
- a few cues

The player reviews the card and mentally rehearses the four moments:

- when we have it
- when we lose it
- when they have it
- when we win it

Players picture the moments; they do not memorize a checklist. The tactical detail lives in this manual.

The last session before a match should be shorter and sharper: small-sided games, role reminders, set pieces. Nothing brand new.

Matchday instruction should mostly be reminders, not first-time teaching.

## 31. Individual development plans

Every player, starter or reserve, should regularly know:

- one current strength to build on
- one current development priority
- what improvement would look like

These are not permanent labels and should be updated as players improve.

No player on the bench should be confused about what she needs to improve to earn greater opportunity.

### The conversation

1. Ask her first: "What do you think your next priority is?"
2. Add the coach's view.
3. Agree on one priority, written as one observable habit.
4. Name what she works on in team training and what she works on outside practice (see the [Home Development Menu](home-development.md)).
5. Name her next test: a game, activity, or moment where she will try it.
6. Check the four corners: soccer, physical, mental, and social.
7. Revisit it roughly once a month.

Give development feedback face to face at the field, in view of others. Afterward, the player writes her strength, priority, and what progress looks like in her own words on the [Development Plan](templates/development-plan.md) and brings it to office hours at the field.

Example priority: **Want the moment.** One pressure rep each week that she chooses (a practice penalty, a free kick, first voice in a huddle), using her routine. Improvement looks like stepping forward and keeping the routine, not scoring every time.

### Role and minutes questions

Invite players to ask about roles and minutes: "What would I need to show to earn that?" Answer with one or two observable things and a time to check back. The player does the talking. A parent can help her prepare and may be present. Talks happen in view of others, and every message includes her parent (§44). Welcome specific development requests, and answer with a yes, a not-yet with a clear path, or a training alternative. The player-facing version is [Asking for opportunities](team-culture-and-leadership.md#asking-for-opportunities).

## 32. Player ownership and peer learning

Players should help each other learn, but should not grade one another.

**Help each other learn. Don't grade each other.**

Encourage:

- questions
- shared observations
- demonstrations
- useful information
- specific praise

Discourage:

- blame
- bossiness
- public criticism
- acting as another player's authority

Structured peer feedback happens face to face, in coach-led settings such as film sessions or practice debriefs. Praise can be public. Corrections stay small and specific, and major ones come from the coach.

**Peer feedback lives on the field, not in the group chat.**

The player-facing version is [Help each other learn](team-culture-and-leadership.md#help-each-other-learn).

Coaches should also periodically ask players what team problem they see and how they might solve it. Give players chances to lead warm-ups, explain principles, and organize small groups ([Team Culture: Everyone leads](team-culture-and-leadership.md#everyone-leads)).

## 33. Team development culture

**Your effort is part of your teammate's training.**

Full-speed defending, transition effort, movement, communication, and honest competition create better repetitions for everyone. Growth happens fastest when the team develops together.

Players hear it as: **We compete hard against each other to make each other better, not to rank each other.**

In practice:

- Keep training games competitive and scored.
- Mix up the teams.
- Avoid running leaderboards that rank teammates.
- For individual work, let players chase their own personal bests.

The player-facing explanation is in [Team Culture](team-culture-and-leadership.md#your-effort-is-part-of-your-teammates-training).

## 34. Technical practice detail

### Passing

- firm when appropriate
- usually on the ground
- targeted to a specific foot or useful space
- played to the foot that tells the receiver which way to turn
- weighted to help the next action

A pass should not merely arrive. It should help the receiver.

**The pass tells the receiver which way to turn.**

### Receiving

Train:

- scanning before arrival
- open body shape
- receiving across the body
- first touch away from pressure
- receiving into the next action
- turning when possible
- protecting when necessary

A good first touch is not merely stopping the ball. It is using the arriving ball to begin the next action.

**Receiving under pressure is a skill to develop, not a situation to avoid.**

Players who hide from pressure in training will not solve it in games.

### Coaching scanning

Coach scanning by naming what to find, not just saying "scan":

- Where is your open teammate?
- Where is the nearest opponent?
- Where is the space?

Give the cue before small-sided games and in short breaks, then let them play. Train it inside games, not only in drills.

**First standard: at least one look before receiving whenever there is time.** More looks are not automatically better. What she picks up and uses matters most. This evidence comes from female players aged about 16–18, so its transfer to younger players is untested. For the player-facing receiving posture, see [Communication & Movement Fundamentals](communication-and-movement.md#receiving-posture).

## 35. Maximum meaningful ball interaction

More meaningful ball interactions generally create more opportunities for development.

Positions do not get equal touches in games. A center back may get a handful while central midfielders get many. At this age, repetitions matter, so training, small-sided games, and rotation should give low-touch roles extra chances to receive, turn, carry, and combine.

Warm-ups should minimize unnecessary lines, waiting, and dead balls.

Standard:

**BALL ALIVE. FEET ALIVE. BRAIN ALIVE.**

- **Play small-sided games to goals in every session.** They are not a reward saved for the end. (German Football Association guidance through U16: at least 48 net minutes a week per player in small-sided game forms.)
- Watch standing time. If players wait more than they play, change the setup.

Static passing still has a place when a specific technical objective requires it, but ordinary warm-ups should keep players active and engaged. The one planned exception is the injury-prevention warm-up (§36).

## 36. Athletic development

Players should progressively develop:

- acceleration
- deceleration
- change of direction
- coordination
- balance
- strength
- mobility
- repeat sprint ability
- jumping/landing
- lower-leg stiffness and elasticity

Use age-appropriate plyometrics with emphasis on quality, landing control, and sensible workload.

Athletic work should support soccer, never be used as punishment.

For pain, injuries, and coming back after time out, see [Injuries, Recovery, and Return to Play](injuries-and-return-to-play.md).

### Injury-prevention warm-up

This is the exception to the ball-alive warm-up standard (§35).

At least twice a week, all season and in the off-season, the warm-up is a full neuromuscular program: FIFA 11+, or a similar proven program such as Knee Control or PEP. For the youngest players, use 11+ Kids or keep 11+ at level 1.

- It takes about 20 minutes, with every exercise done correctly.
- Teach level 1 until technique is clean, then progress.
- Before games, use the running parts only (Parts 1 and 3).

In teenage girls, programs like this cut ACL injuries by roughly half. **They only work if we actually do them.**

### Team plyometric routine

Build a short, low-volume block into practice warm-ups on a regular schedule:

- pogo jumps
- line hops
- snap-downs
- controlled two-leg jumps
- single-leg hops once landings are sound

Coach the outcome:

- **Land soft and quiet.** Stick the landing before the next jump.
- **Knees stay in line over the toes. Don't let them cave in.**
- On cuts: plant, sink the hips, push off with the knee over the foot.
- Quality over quantity.

**Count good reps, not minutes.**

- Use a few short sets of crisp reps per drill, with full rest between sets.
- Jump first in the session, while players are fresh.
- Stop when landings get loud or knees cave in.
- In a heavy week, jumping is the first thing to cut.

On injury-prevention warm-up days, the warm-up's jumping exercises count as the plyometric block.

Progress volume gradually. Players who want more can use the [Home Development Menu](home-development.md), which uses the same cues.

### Growth and maturation

**Same age, different body age.** Girls usually grow fastest around 11–12, but anywhere from about 9 to 15 is normal. On a U13–U14 roster, some players haven't started their growth spurt, some are in it, and some are done.

- During a fast growth phase, expect some temporary clumsiness. Keep teaching landing, balance, and strength, because this age is when that work does the most for knees. Ease off extra volume if she has heel or knee pain.
- Don't mistake early size for talent, or late growth for lack of it. This matters for positions and playing time (§29).

**Talk about what bodies can do, never how they look.**

Praise strong, fast, balanced, and brave. No comments about weight, shape, or appearance from coaches, parents, or teammates.

## 37. Heading and head injuries

**Head with purpose, not habit.**

High-value situations include:

- scoring
- saving a goal
- defending dangerous crosses
- redirecting dangerous balls
- situations where heading is clearly the best tactical action

Players are not obligated to attack every high ball merely because it can be headed. Routine punts do not automatically require an aerial challenge. Beyond high-value situations, whether to head the ball is the player's choice.

### Heading limits

Training exposure should remain age-appropriate and consistent with current governing-body guidance. US Club Soccer, which sanctions ECNL, sets heading rules by age group:

- **U13:** Heading practice is capped at 30 minutes and 15–20 headers per player per week. There are no limits in games.
- **U14 and older:** There are no limits, but we still head with purpose, not habit.
- **Playing up:** An 11- or 12-year-old playing up at U14 still follows the U13 practice limits.

Set-piece rehearsals count toward the limit, so walk through runs without live headers when needed. Age groups now follow the Aug 1–Jul 31 school year. Confirm each player's age group, and check current club and league policy, each season.

### Teaching aerial duels

**In heading situations, most concussions come from contact with another player, not from the ball.** When heading is the right choice, teach it well:

- technique first at low ball speed (a lighter ball is fine for learning)
- an early call and a committed jump, or leave it: no late, half-hearted jumps
- arms for balance and space, never elbows
- never jumping into a teammate's or opponent's back

Girls' soccer reports concussions at nearly twice the rate of boys'. Take every head knock seriously.

### Head injuries

**When in doubt, sit her out.**

After any blow to the head or body, watch for:

- dazed, confused, slow to answer, or can't remember what just happened
- unsteady, clumsier than usual, or a change in mood or behavior
- headache, dizziness, nausea, vision problems, or bothered by light or noise
- feeling "foggy" or "not right"

Any of these: she comes out now. If no health care professional is on site to evaluate her, she is done for the day. (ECNL allows a free temporary substitution while an on-site health care professional evaluates her; only that person can clear her back in.)

Call 911 for a seizure, loss of consciousness, repeated vomiting, a headache that gets worse, slurred speech, weakness or numbness, unequal pupils, or not recognizing people or places.

After a concussion, no contact, heading, full training, or games until a health care professional clears her. Never ask a player to "shake it off."

### Returning after a concussion

Returning after a concussion is a medical process, not a coaching decision:

- the first 1–2 days: relative rest, light walking, and less screen time
- then step by step (light exercise → soccer drills without contact → full training), at least 24 hours per step, guided by her health care provider, stepping back and calling the provider if symptoms return
- medical clearance (in writing where the state requires it) before any contact, heading, or full training

Most teens recover within a month. If headaches, dizziness, or neck pain last more than about 10 days, the family should go back to her health care provider.

Other injuries and the return to play are covered in [Injuries, Recovery, and Return to Play](injuries-and-return-to-play.md).

## 38. Safety basics

- Keep an emergency action plan for each venue: field access for EMS, the nearest AED, and copies with every coach, reviewed each season.
- Coaches are trained in CPR and AED use.
- Heat, hydration, lightning, and air quality follow club policy and U.S. Soccer's Recognize to Recover guidance. For lightning, wait 30 minutes after the last thunder or lightning.
- Guidance changes, so check it each season.

## 39. Film study

Players should learn to evaluate their own decisions rather than simply ask whether they played well.

Use the review sequence in the [Film Review Guide](film-review-guide.md#review-sequence): pause before the outcome, scan the picture, list the realistic options, play each one forward 2–4 actions, look for a non-obvious or deceptive solution, choose before pressing play, watch what actually happened, judge the decision and the execution separately, and write down one useful lesson. The guide also has one question for each position and a simple game tally that players record on the [Game Reflection](templates/game-reflection.md) sheet.

### Team film share

Every player watches a game. At the next practice, go around the group: each player names one thing she needs to work on.

- Players talk only about their own game.
- Each names a behavior, not a feeling.
- Everyone describes rather than judges, and nobody grades teammates.

The coach connects the answers to upcoming training and each player's development priority (§31). Team film stays with the team; see [Sharing film safely](film-review-guide.md#sharing-film-safely).

## 40. Practice design should respond to games

A club curriculum can provide structure, but it should not replace coaching.

If matches reveal recurring problems, upcoming training should address them.

Examples in possession:

- automatic vertical play
- poor first touch under pressure
- panicking under pressure instead of supporting possession
- passing short until trapped, then clearing blindly
- no intentional passes beyond short rondo distances
- inability to switch
- poor midfield spacing
- players hiding after passing

Examples in transition, defending, and communication:

- weak transition reactions
- inability to defend 1v1
- lack of communication
- relying on one player to organize the back line

The recurring coaching question should be:

**What problem from our games are we teaching them to solve?**

Players should be able to answer their version: "What soccer problem did today's practice teach me to solve?" Answers might be escaping pressure, playing out of the back, creating a 2v1, finding someone between the lines, switching the field, defending a 2v2, pressing, supporting the ball, or turning under pressure. If players cannot name the problem week after week, the practice is not teaching clearly enough.

### Session shape: Play → Practice → Play

U.S. Soccer's Play-Practice-Play model gives a simple session shape:

1. **Play:** small-sided games as players arrive, linked to today's problem. The coach mostly watches, adding brief praise, key words, or a question.
2. **Practice:** a directional, game-like activity in the part of the field where the problem happens. Make it easier or harder until players both succeed and fail.
3. **Play:** a bigger game with few stoppages. Use one short break to ask what players noticed.

Finish by checking whether players can say what problem the session worked on.

## 41. Game-based learning and constraints

Training should contain realistic teammates, opponents, direction, space, pressure, transition, and consequences whenever practical.

### Most of practice should look like soccer

Observational studies of youth coaches (all with boys' teams in England) found most practice time went to drills, technique, and fitness with no game context. Skill-learning research suggests moving more time into game-like activities, without throwing out technique work.

Quick check for any activity:

- **Organized:** Is it safe, and does the ball stay in play?
- **Game-like:** Does it include attacking, defending, and transition?
- **Repetition:** Are there many reps of today's problem?
- **Challenging:** Do players both succeed and fail?
- **Coached:** Is the coaching the right amount for the age and level?

If an activity has no opponent, no direction, or no decision, **keep it short and connect it to a game soon after.**

### Constraints

**Keep the game picture. Change the problem.**

A good constraint keeps what players read in real games (opponents, teammates, direction, space). It changes a rule, the space, the numbers, or the scoring so one solution becomes more valuable.

Constraints can temporarily emphasize a principle:

- no forward passes
- touch limits
- required switches
- bonus points for carries
- goals after third-player combinations
- scoring after changing fields

How long they last:

- Keep bans (such as no forward passes, §6) short. A long ban removes a picture players must learn to read.
- Rewards (bonus points for switches, carries, third-player goals) can run longer.
- Specific rules should fade. Shaping the problem through space, numbers, and scoring never stops.

Run the constraint, lift it, then ask the question. For example, play ten minutes of possession with no forward passes, then remove the rule and ask: **When is the forward pass actually better?**

The goal is not players who obey artificial rules. The goal is players who recognize the underlying soccer problem independently. Over time, good decisions should become automatic through pattern recognition: repeated realistic pictures in training build the patterns, and games show whether they stuck.

## 42. Create advantages

Teach players to recognize and create:

- **numerical advantage** — 2v1, 3v2
- **positional advantage** — receiving between lines
- **spatial advantage** — open grass
- **qualitative advantage** — isolating a player in a matchup she can exploit

Ask:

**Where is our advantage? If none exists, how can we create one?**

## 43. Coach communication

A core coaching question:

**What did you see?**

This encourages players to explain perception and intention rather than simply defend themselves after a mistake.

Questions only help if players are really thinking:

- Ask open questions: "What did you see? What else was possible?"
- Give think time, and accept more than one good answer.
- Ask at natural breaks, not in the middle of a rep.
- Still show and tell when a player doesn't have the information to solve the problem.

Short cues and pictures ("Make a defender choose") tend to survive game pressure better than long technical explanations.

The best coaching should progressively make the coach less necessary.

## 44. Communication, availability, and safeguarding

**Open. Observable. About soccer.**

**Club safeguarding policy comes first.** Every adult working with the team, parent volunteers included, keeps SafeSport training and background screening current and follows club and US Club Soccer rules. If this handbook and club policy ever differ, club policy wins; check it each season. Safety or abuse concerns are reported immediately, never saved for office hours: 911 in an emergency, local authorities for suspected child abuse, and the U.S. Center for SafeSport (833-587-7233 or its online form).

### Coach availability

- The coach stays 15 minutes after every practice. A player can book up to 5 minutes, in person or through the team app with a parent included.
- Film questions come through the team app with a parent included. Other topics are handled in person.
- Parents with a concern about a match wait 24 hours, then ask for a conversation. Safety, health, or welfare concerns are raised right away.
- Talks happen at the field, in view of others. **Out of earshot is fine. Out of sight is not.**
- Parents are welcome to listen in. No talks in cars or closed rooms.
- End office hours while other adults are still at the field. No player waits alone with one coach for her ride.

No player should have to guess how to ask a question.

### Messages

- Use one club-approved team app. Parent accounts are the main contact, players join only if the app's age rules allow, and parents stay on every thread.
- Every coach message to a player includes her parent. If a player writes first, add a parent before replying.
- No direct messages on social media, no disappearing-message apps, and no personal social-media connections with players.
- Messages stay about soccer and go out between 8 a.m. and 8 p.m. unless there is a schedule or safety need.
- A parent's written request to stop electronic contact is honored.

### Travel

Carpools and transport follow club policy. No player rides alone with one unrelated adult without the parent's advance written permission, and parents pick up their own child first and drop her off last when possible.

The family-facing summary is in [Parent Partnership](parent-partnership.md#communication-and-safeguarding).

## 45. Team identity

We want a team recognized for players who:

- scan constantly
- communicate
- play with intention
- train at speed
- remain calm in games
- react immediately in transition
- want the moment and step into pressure moments
- support teammates
- take ownership of their development

With the ball, they:

- demand and support the ball
- use deception
- move after passing
- carry open space
- switch play
- exploit weak-side space
- understand third-player combinations
- recognize when the game is small and when it is big

Without the ball, they:

- defend collectively
- delay rather than dive in
- use good pursuit angles

Above everything:

**NO HIDING. NO WATCHING. THINK, MOVE, INFLUENCE THE GAME.**
