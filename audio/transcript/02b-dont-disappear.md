# Don't Disappear: Playing Your Game in a Different Environment

This chapter is different from the rest of this audiobook.

Most of what you've heard so far describes how we want to play, and how good coaching teaches it. This one is for a situation you might be in right now. You understand the soccer you want to play. But the environment you're playing in makes it hard to show.

Maybe the game around you feels rushed. Maybe mistakes make everyone tense. Maybe the qualities that come naturally to you, like staying calm, or looking for a connection, seem less useful here than they do somewhere else.

That's hard. And it's worth being clear about what it does and doesn't mean. It doesn't mean you're a worse player than you thought. It means the picture around you is different, and you need a way to stay yourself inside it.

Later in this audiobook, the How We Play chapter talks about how to recognize what an environment might be missing. This chapter is about something else: what you do while you're still in it. On the field, with your teammates, and in your own head.

Here's where it's going. [pause] Don't disappear. Trust your game. Read the moment.

Let's start with the feeling that you don't fit.

Picture this. Everyone around you plays fast. The ball comes to you, and you take a touch to look up, because you saw a teammate getting free. Before you've even played the pass, you hear it: "Hurry up!" And in that moment, being calm feels like being slow.

It happens in other parts of the game too. If everyone plays forward the second they get the ball, looking for a connection can feel hesitant. If everyone dives into tackles, delaying an attacker can feel passive.

But notice where those feelings come from. They come from comparing yourself to the people around you. They don't tell you whether your decision was good. Maybe you read the picture well. Or maybe there really was a reason to go sooner, and you missed it. The only way to know is to look at the actual picture: the pressure, the space, your teammates, and the danger.

Do not confuse fitting in with contributing.

Sometimes the thing that makes you feel different is one of the most valuable things you bring. Calm matters most when everyone is rushing. Connection matters most when players are isolated. And when teammates are afraid of the next mistake, one player who still wants the ball can help everyone else play.

That doesn't mean you ignore what the game needs. A team that plays directly still needs someone supporting under the ball, players ready for second balls, and cover against a counter. A team that keeps passing sideways may need someone to carry into open space, or make a run in behind. Your job is to see what helps in this moment, without throwing away what makes you valuable.

So ask two questions, in this order. First: What do I do well, and how can I use it here? Second: What does this moment need? [long pause]

The order matters. Adapt your actions to the game. But don't reinvent yourself every time the team has a weakness.

Now, a different kind of value.

Some actions get noticed. A goal. A big tackle. A long sprint. Other actions change a game without anyone saying a word.

Picture this. An attacker breaks on a counter, and you're the only one back. You don't dive in. You slow her down, and slow her down, until a teammate gets back to help. Nobody cheers. But you may have just saved a goal.

Or you make a run that creates an outlet, and the pass never comes. You play the simple pass that lets the next player break a line. You protect the middle while everyone else attacks. You tell a teammate she has time. You show for the ball again, right after you lost it.

None of those make a highlight. That doesn't make them unimportant. Just be honest with yourself, because an unused run or a simple pass isn't automatically good either. Ask what it made possible, or what it protected.

Feeling valuable is not the same as being valuable. Learn to recognize a useful contribution, even when nobody points it out. The film chapter later on shows you how to watch for this kind of influence away from the ball.

And leadership isn't always being the loudest player, or making the biggest play. Sometimes it's bringing your best qualities, consistently, when the game around you gets difficult.

Here's something a lot of players notice.

You play pickup, or futsal, or a scrimmage with a different group. And suddenly you're a different player. You talk more. You ask for the ball. You control the pace. Teammates look for you. You feel useful.

That player didn't suddenly appear. She was there all along. That environment just made it easier to use qualities you already had.

So what can you carry back? Scanning. Showing again. Giving information. Committing to a decision. You may have to adjust them. A turn that worked when you had more time might need to become a quick set pass and another movement against faster pressure. But the behaviors come with you.

Remember: don't wait to feel important before you influence the game. Bring your game into it.

Let's talk about mistakes, because that's where a lot of confidence gets lost.

Confidence doesn't mean believing every decision you make is right. Real confidence lets you look at a mistake honestly, without treating it as a verdict on your ability, or on your future.

A mistake can change what you do next without changing what you believe about yourself.

You can play a bad pass and still ask for the next one. You can get beaten and still want the next duel. You can hear a correction, admit you missed something, and keep trusting your ability to learn.

It helps to have a few words ready for that moment. Here's one version. [pause] [slows down] Still me. Learn. Next play.

"Still me" means one action doesn't erase your strengths, your work, or your progress. "Learn" means take the useful information. Was the choice wrong, or the execution, or both? And "next play" means get back into the game, and make yourself useful again.

Short self-talk cues like that really can help performance. And research finds that words athletes choose for themselves work about as well as words someone hands them. So if different words fit you better, use yours.

Those words sit underneath the team mantra you already know. Say the next-play mantra with me. [pause] [slows down] Respond. Reflect. Learn. Next play.

During play, respond first. Recover, cover, communicate, or become an option again. The deeper reflecting waits for a break, or for film. And you use the same team reset as always. This isn't another routine to memorize.

Here's something that might surprise you. Secure confidence actually makes you more coachable. If a mistake doesn't threaten who you are, you can say, "I made the wrong decision," without concluding, "I should stop making decisions."

Doubt will still show up sometimes: after a mistake, after a correction, or during a rough few weeks. That's normal. It just doesn't get to make the next decision for you.

Try this. Think of a teammate you respect. [pause] Picture her making one bad pass. [pause] You wouldn't decide she'd suddenly become a bad player. You'd help her see what happened, and you'd help her get ready for the next action. [long pause] Now give yourself the same thing: honesty and support, together.

That isn't just being nice to yourself. In one study, self-critical women athletes who practiced treating their own mistakes this way became less self-critical, and worried less about making mistakes.

Now the harder part. When you're afraid of criticism, hiding starts to feel safe.

From the outside, it rarely looks like hiding. You stop asking for the ball. You stay behind an opponent instead of moving into an angle. You go quiet instead of talking. You stop making your own reads and start waiting to be told what to do. You leave every hard decision to someone else. And for a while, it works, because fewer people notice your mistakes.

If you've felt that pull, you're not unusual. A lot of players have.

But here's the cost. The game loses what you could have given it. And you get fewer chances to practice the exact actions that would help you trust yourself again.

It can turn into a loop. Confidence drops, so you hide. You hide, so you influence the game less. You influence it less, so you feel less valuable, and confidence drops again.

You don't break that loop by suddenly taking over the game. You break it with one useful action.

Picture it. [pause] [slows down] You've just lost the ball in midfield. For a second, you want to disappear. Instead, you sprint back, and help the team get compact. [long pause] A minute later, we win it back. You move into a clear passing lane, and you show for the ball. [long pause] That's it. You're back in the game.

One useful action can be small. Move into a clear passing lane when your role allows it. Give a teammate information she can't see. After you pass, find another angle. Commit to the next appropriate duel or decision.

You don't have to demand every pass, force a risky turn, or leave your position to prove you're brave. Sometimes protecting space is the most useful thing you can do. The real question is whether your choice serves the game, or mainly keeps you out of view.

Courage is not only making the difficult play. Courage is staying available after the difficult play fails.

Some players hear "stay calm" and think it means playing at half speed. It doesn't.

Say the game mantra with me. [pause] [slows down] Calm mind. Fast soccer.

Calm thinking goes with full effort. Sprint to recover. Compete for the ball. Move early and decisively. You can do all of that and still keep enough attention to see what comes next. Body intense. Brain calm.

Good players also change the pace of what they do as the picture changes. Sometimes the moment calls for organizing: scanning, talking, protecting space, or delaying while help arrives. Sometimes it calls for connecting: supporting, combining, and moving to a new angle. And sometimes it calls for exploding: attacking a gap, pressing a poor touch, sprinting in behind, or making a recovery run.

Those aren't fixed settings. A patient moment can set up an explosive one. And a fast approach may need a controlled finish.

Defending shows this clearly. Picture a counterattack. An attacker is running at you, you're isolated, and a teammate is sprinting back to help. A desperate tackle might win the ball. But if you get beaten, she has a clear path to goal. So approach quickly, then slow your final steps, get balanced, and protect the dangerous route. Your delay buys time for your teammate to arrive and cover. You've made the situation better without touching the ball.

And keep looking for the moment to win it. A heavy touch, an exposed ball, or a teammate arriving in a good covering spot may let you step in decisively. Backing off isn't automatically safe either, if she's close enough to shoot or play a dangerous pass.

Remember: approach fast, defend patiently, attack the opportunity.

Delay and aggression belong in the same defender. The skill is knowing which one the picture is asking for.

What about when a coach's instruction doesn't make sense to you?

Often, it makes sense once you understand the problem behind it. The coach may have seen a runner, a pressing trigger, or a danger you missed. And sometimes, you saw something worth talking about too.

During the game, listen, do your job, and keep playing. Then, at a break or afterward, ask about one specific moment. Something like: "I delayed because I was isolated and waiting for cover. What would you want me to recognize before stepping?"

Then compare the two pictures. What did I see, and what was I trying to do? What was the coach asking for, and why? What information did I miss? What does the film show about the real options? And what will I try next time?

Sometimes the coach was right. Sometimes more than one choice was reasonable. Sometimes the film changes both of your minds. Either way, the goal is to learn the principle behind the instruction, not to win the argument.

A correction should help you answer, "What can I change?" Not, "What does this prove about me?"

Be coachable. Think independently. Understand why. Notice what is missing. Keep developing anyway.

And if a pattern keeps bothering you, week after week, that's different from one confusing instruction. Bring it to office hours, the way the team culture chapter described. A different style, a missing teaching point, and a culture that makes players afraid are three different problems. Respond to the one you're actually facing.

Here's an idea that changes how all of this feels. The environment isn't something only coaches create. Every player helps create it. Including you.

Your effort shapes practice. Your communication changes what your teammates know. And the way you react to a teammate's mistake affects whether she's willing to try again.

Confidence can spread, too. In an experiment with newly formed soccer teams, when the team's leader expressed high confidence in the team, teammates became more confident, and the team played better than teams whose leader showed less confidence. And whether or not you wear the armband, your reactions, your communication, and your support all help shape the environment around you.

So if you know something useful, share it. Good teammates teach each other. Sometimes that's encouragement. Sometimes it's information. And sometimes it's asking, respectfully, "Can I show you what I'm seeing?" Then be just as willing to learn from her.

Help each other learn. Don't grade each other.

In games, keep it short and useful. If a teammate makes a mistake, say "Next play," then give her cover or an option. If a good idea fails in the execution, recognize the idea, then deal with the new situation. If she can't see pressure coming, give her an accurate call: "Time," "Turn," or "Man on." If the defense needs help, use the shared cues: "Step," "Drop," or "Delay." If a teammate makes a great run and nobody finds her, tell her it was a good run. And if she's unsure about an idea, ask whether she wants to work through it together at a break.

Your words should help someone play. Repeated correction, public blame, and trying to prove you know more all make that harder.

When you think back on a game, one useful question is: Did I make somebody else's game easier? [long pause]

And after a teammate's mistake, one message matters more than any advice: you're still with us. You usually don't need to say those words. You show them. You talk to her normally, give her the next option, trust her with the ball again, and keep including her.

Here's why that matters so much. When you keep responding well to mistakes, talking clearly, asking for the ball, and helping instead of grading, other players may start doing the same. Culture is built through repeated small actions. Blame can become normal. But so can recovery, support, and courage. You can't control which habits spread. But your actions are part of what becomes normal.

There's a limit, though, and it matters. You can create a passing option, but you can't make the pass come. You can share an idea, but you can't make a teammate use it. You can organize, model a habit, and ask a good question, but a whole-team change may still need the coach's teaching.

So do your part. If practice needs intensity, raise yours. If teammates are getting negative, give them a different response. If a shared problem needs teaching, take it to the coach with a specific example. And if your first suggestion doesn't help, listen, and adjust.

Take responsibility for your contribution, without taking ownership of every outcome. A team problem that isn't solved yet doesn't mean you failed to lead. Keep contributing where you can. And when a problem needs more help, use the same process as any other question: office hours at the field, or a message through the team app with a parent included.

Every so often, it helps to collect some evidence about the player you're becoming.

Think of one specific example in three areas. [pause] Courage: when did you do something useful, even though you might fail? [long pause] Intelligence: when did you recognize or solve a soccer problem? [long pause] And leadership: when did you help someone else play, or recover? [long pause]

Maybe it was showing for the ball right after losing it. Maybe it was delaying a counterattack until cover arrived. Maybe it was giving a nervous teammate a clear outlet.

Be honest about what happened, and about what still needs work. But keep the examples. Specific examples give your confidence something real to stand on, in a way general reassurance never does. They help you see more than your last mistake.

This isn't another scorecard to fill in after every game. Do it every so often. And if you use film, make staying involved after mistakes your theme for one session.

Your current team and position matter. But they don't have to give you every soccer experience you need.

If you rarely get to receive under pressure, or play through the middle, find places that keep giving you those pictures: small-sided games, pickup, futsal, or another suitable training group. And study players in other positions. Follow one player, pause before her next action, predict what she'll do, and see if you were right.

Different groups teach different things. One might give you time to practice taking responsibility. Another might force you to scan earlier and combine faster. Notice what each one teaches, and bring it back to your team.

Just don't pile it on. Use the home development menu selectively, and fit everything around team training, school, sleep, and recovery. Filling a gap doesn't mean adding work every day.

And some soccer should simply be yours. Play without judging every action. Experiment. Lose the ball. Laugh. Try again. You don't need a lesson from every game with your friends.

Games with no adult in charge have their own value, too. You have to pick teams, settle small disagreements, communicate, decide what to try, and adapt, without waiting to be told. In one study, elite English academy players who went on to become professionals had spent more time in informal soccer play between the ages of six and twelve than players who didn't, but not more time in organized practice.

Your coach shapes your environment. You still own your development.

So let's put it together.

When the game gets chaotic, don't start by asking how to become more like everyone else. And don't try to personally fix every weakness on the team.

Start with what you do well. [pause] Can you give the team a clear passing option? See the next play early? Connect two players? Control the pace? Communicate? Protect space? Make a teammate's decision easier? [long pause]

Use those strengths with confidence. Then read the moment, and adapt.

Sometimes the team will be missing something that isn't one of your biggest strengths. Help where you can. Compete. Do your job. Keep developing the weaker parts of your game. But don't throw away the qualities that make you valuable just because the environment is missing something else.

Help with what is missing. Don't become what is missing.

A good player adapts her actions without losing her identity. The same technical, intelligent player might play quickly in one moment, slow the next one down, make a hard recovery run, or choose a direct pass. Adapting changes the solution. It doesn't change the player you're becoming.

You may not get the pass. You may not get credit. You may choose well and execute badly. You may find out your choice was wrong. Learn from it, and stay involved.
