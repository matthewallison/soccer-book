# Communication and Movement Fundamentals

Shared language makes the team faster, because nobody has to guess what a teammate means.

The goal is not constant shouting. Communication should be early, specific, useful, and positive.

The communication glossary.

We use three kinds of calls. Listen closely, because there's a quick quiz at the end.

The first kind is information calls. These give a teammate information, rather than making the decision for her.

"Time" means you have room to control the ball and look up.

"Man on" means pressure is arriving right now.

"Turn" means you can receive and face forward.

"Left shoulder" or "right shoulder" means check that side, now.

"Inside" or "outside" tells her where the pressure, support, or space is. For example, support is available inside.

"Cover" means I'm behind or inside you, as support.

And "line" means there's an option down the touchline.

The second kind is action calls. These suggest a useful action, but the player who hears the call still owns the final decision.

"Set" means bounce or lay the ball back quickly.

"Hold" means keep possession, and don't force the ball forward.

"Drop" means recover, or offer a deeper support option.

"Step" means close down or intercept, now.

"Delay" means buy time, and don't dive in.

And "leave" means let the ball run to another player.

The third kind is big-space calls. These tell the team the field has opened. Anyone can call them, but the player on the ball still owns the decision.

"Expand" means we've escaped pressure, so use the larger space now.

"Switch" means change the point of attack to the weak side, the side away from the ball.

"Carry" means there's open grass, so attack it with the ball.

And "go" means there's an immediate transition or breakaway opportunity.

Over time, choose useful information over remote-control commands.

Changing the size of the game.

Recognize whether the problem in front of you is a small-space problem or a large-space problem.

For a small-space problem, use quick support, short combinations, third-player actions, wall passes, tight receiving, and deception.

For a large-space problem, use carrying, longer passing, switches, runs beyond the defense, weak-side movement, and acceleration into open grass.

When we escape pressure, the problem has changed. That's the moment to call "Expand" and use the bigger space.

Say it with me. [pause] [slows down] Small space: combine. Big space: exploit.

Receiving posture.

Scan while the ball travels. The best time to look away is when nothing new can happen to the ball, like while it travels between two teammates. Look early to build the picture. Look again just before the ball arrives. And look again after your first touch.

Try it right now, wherever you are. [pause] Check your left shoulder. [pause] Now your right. [pause] That's it. Quick looks are enough. You don't need to stare.

When you look, know what you're looking for. Where is my open teammate? Where is the nearest opponent? Where is the space? The first standard is at least one look before you receive, whenever there's time. More looks aren't automatically better. What you pick up and use matters most.

Be side-on when possible. Open your hips enough to see both where the ball came from and where your next action might go. But don't force an exaggerated side-on position if pressure or the angle of the pass makes another posture safer.

Meet the ball. Don't wait flat-footed for a pass a defender can attack. Step toward the pass when it makes sense, and arrive before the defender does. Meeting the ball applies when you check to feet, when you receive in midfield, when you intercept as a defender, and when you attack crosses and loose balls.

Your first touch begins the next action. A good first touch is not simply stopping the ball. Use the arriving ball to turn, escape pressure, carry, protect, or set up your next pass.

Your first touch can beat a defender. A touch past or away from an arriving defender can take her out of the play before you ever pass or dribble.

And when you're the passer, remember: the pass tells the receiver which way to turn. Play it firm when that helps, usually on the ground, to the foot or the space that sets up her next action.

Receiving under pressure is a skill to develop, not a situation to avoid.

Athletic posture.

Knees soft. Weight balanced. Feet alive. Not tall and locked. Ready to move in more than one direction.

Defending one-on-one.

Approach fast, arrive controlled. Close the space quickly, then shorten your final steps so the attacker can't beat you with one touch.

Low, wide, and mobile. As you arrive, break down: feet a little wider than your shoulders, knees bent, weight on the balls of your feet. Get low and wide enough to move side to side, but don't sink so low that your movement becomes slow.

Avoid crossing your feet when you don't need to. Use controlled side-to-side steps and crossover steps, depending on the attacker's distance and speed.

Show, rather than dive. Use your body angle to steer the attacker toward less dangerous space.

When the attacker has the advantage, you may need to buy time rather than win the ball right away. The sequence is five words. Say them with me. [pause] [slows down] Protect. Slow. Show. Wait. Win.

Protect: stay between the attacker and the most dangerous route to goal.

Slow: take away her speed.

Show: steer her toward less dangerous space, usually away from the center.

Wait: stay balanced. Don't dive in just because the ball is nearby.

Win: attack the ball when her touch is poor, she loses her balance, help arrives, the space disappears, or the challenge turns in your favor.

An excellent delay may contain no tackle at all.

Pursuit angles.

Picture this. [pause] An attacker has broken past you, and she's heading toward our goal. Where do you run? [long pause]

Don't run to where the attacker is. Run to where she is going.

Aim first toward the dangerous lane between the attacker and the goal, then curve your recovery run as the gap closes. That cuts off central space, shortens your route to goal, and forces the attacker wider. And it means you don't have to win a pure footrace from behind.

Movement timing.

Move on the pass. Move again after you pass.

Moving on the pass means anticipating the next picture while the ball is traveling. Start moving as the pass is played, not after it arrives.

Moving again after you pass means asking yourself, every time: Where can I help next?

Arrive, don't camp. If you get to a useful space too early, defenders can settle and block the lane. Move into the space as the passing lane opens up.

Different heights. Avoid standing in a flat line with nearby teammates. Staggering, with some of you higher up the field and some deeper, creates angles and options for a third player.

Defensive vision.

On defense, gather information about the ball, the goal, your teammates, your opponents, and dangerous space.

See the ball and the most dangerous space or opponent. Don't get so focused on a single player that you lose the bigger picture.

Protect dangerous space first. Get tighter as the ball and the danger get closer.

Get compact first, then pick up the dangerous opponent in your part of the field. Get tight on restarts, like free kicks, corners, and throw-ins. Get tight on crosses, and when you step to win the ball.

Transition posture.

Good defensive positioning should help the next attack. And good attacking positioning should help the next defensive transition.

Defend from a position you can attack from.

The final standard.

Good movement should make your teammates' decisions easier. Say it with me. [pause] [slows down] Create an angle. Meet the ball. Stay balanced. Move again.
