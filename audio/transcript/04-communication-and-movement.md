# Communication and Movement Fundamentals

This chapter is about two things that make a team faster without anyone running faster: what we say to each other, and how we move.

Start with talking. Shared language makes a team quicker, because nobody has to stop and guess what a teammate means. One clear word can do the work of a whole sentence.

That doesn't mean constant shouting. A team yelling nonstop is just noise, and players learn to tune it out. Good communication is early, specific, useful, and positive. It arrives before the moment, not after it.

We use three kinds of calls, and each one does a different job.

The first kind gives information. It tells a teammate something she can't see, and then leaves the decision to her.

Picture this. Your teammate is receiving with her back to goal, and a defender is sprinting at her from behind. She can't see it. You can. One word: "Man on." Pressure is arriving right now. Now she knows to protect the ball or play it quickly.

Flip the picture. Same teammate, same pass, but the nearest defender is ten yards away. The call is "Time." You have room to control the ball and look up. And if she can receive and face forward, the call is "Turn."

The rest of the information calls work the same way. "Left shoulder" or "right shoulder" tells her to check that side, right now. "Inside" or "outside" tells her where the pressure, the support, or the space is. For example, support is available inside. "Cover" means I'm behind you, or inside you, as support. And "line" means there's an option down the touchline.

The second kind of call suggests an action. The player who hears it still owns the final decision, but you're pointing her toward something useful.

"Set" means bounce the ball back to me, or lay it off, quickly. "Hold" means keep possession and don't force it forward. "Drop" means recover, or offer a deeper option for support.

Some action calls are for defending. Picture a loose pass rolling toward an opponent near you. Your teammate yells "Step." Close her down, or intercept, now. But if an attacker is running at you with space behind you and no help yet, the right call is "Delay." Buy time. Don't dive in. And "leave" means let the ball run through to another player.

The third kind of call is for when the field opens up. These are the big-space calls. Anyone can make them, but the player on the ball still owns the decision.

You've just combined your way out of a tight corner of the field, and suddenly there's grass everywhere. Someone calls "Expand." We've escaped pressure, so use the bigger space now. "Switch" means change the point of attack to the weak side, the side away from the ball. "Carry" means there's open grass in front of you, so attack it with the ball. And "Go" means there's an immediate transition or breakaway on. Go now.

Here's a quick check before we move on. If a teammate yells "Set," what does she want? [long pause] The ball bounced or laid back to her, quickly.

Over time, lean toward information rather than remote control. "Man on" helps a teammate think. "Pass it!" does her thinking for her. The better we get, the more our calls sound like information, and the less they sound like commands.

Now let's connect that to the size of the game.

In the first chapter you heard about small space and big space. Here's why it matters for how we talk and move. Every moment on the ball is either a small-space problem or a large-space problem, and they need different tools.

In a small space, you use quick support, short combinations, wall passes, tight receiving, deception, and third-player actions, where the ball goes through one teammate to reach another. In a large space, you use carrying, longer passes, switches, runs in behind the defense, weak-side movement, and acceleration into open grass.

The mistake is using small-space tools after the problem has changed. When we escape pressure, that's the moment to call "Expand," and use the bigger space.

Remember: small space, combine. Big space, exploit.

Now, receiving the ball, because this is where so much of the game is won or lost.

The best time to look away from the ball is when nothing new can happen to it, like while it's traveling between two teammates. So you scan while the ball travels. Look early, to build the picture. Look again just before the ball arrives. And look again after your first touch.

Try it right now, wherever you are. [pause] Glance over your left shoulder. [pause] Now your right. [pause] That's all a scan is. A quick look. You don't need to stare.

But a scan is only useful if you know what you're looking for. Every time you look, you're answering three questions: Where is my open teammate? Where is the nearest opponent? Where is the space?

The first standard is at least one look before you receive, whenever there's time. More looks aren't automatically better. What matters is what you pick up, and whether you use it.

Your body shape does a lot of the seeing for you. Picture receiving a pass from your center back while you're facing her squarely, flat to the field. All you can see is her and the ground behind her. Now open your hips, side-on, and suddenly you can see both where the ball came from and where it might go next. Side-on when possible. But don't force an exaggerated position if pressure, or the angle of the pass, makes a different posture safer.

Next, meet the ball. Don't wait flat-footed for a pass a defender can attack. Picture a pass coming toward you with a defender just behind your shoulder. If you wait, she steps around you and takes it. If you step toward the pass, you arrive first. This applies all over the field: checking to feet as a forward, receiving in midfield, intercepting as a defender, attacking crosses and loose balls.

And then there's your first touch. A good first touch isn't just stopping the ball. It starts your next action. Use the arriving ball to turn, to escape pressure, to carry, to protect it, or to set up your next pass.

Your first touch can even beat a defender. If she's rushing at you, a touch past her, or away from her, can take her out of the play before you ever pass or dribble.

There's a partner to all of this. When you're the passer, the pass tells the receiver which way to turn. Play it firm when that helps, usually on the ground, to the foot or the space that sets up her next action. A pass to her back foot says "turn away." A pass straight at her says "come back."

And here's a mindset worth holding on to. Receiving under pressure is a skill to develop, not a situation to avoid. The player who hides from pressure in practice won't solve it in games.

Now, your body when you're not on the ball.

Athletic posture is simple to describe. Knees soft. Weight balanced. Feet alive. You're not tall and locked. You're ready to move in more than one direction, because in soccer you rarely know which direction comes next.

That posture matters most when you're defending one-on-one.

Picture an attacker receiving the ball and turning to face you, with fifteen yards between you. You need to close that space quickly. But if you sprint all the way in, she just touches the ball past you. So approach fast, then arrive controlled. Shorten your last few steps so she can't beat you with one touch.

As you arrive, break down. Feet a little wider than your shoulders, knees bent, weight on the balls of your feet. Get low and wide enough to move side to side, but not so low that you're slow. Avoid crossing your feet when you don't need to; use controlled side steps and crossover steps, depending on how far away she is and how fast she's going.

And use your body angle to show her somewhere, instead of diving in. Show her toward less dangerous space.

Sometimes the attacker has the advantage. She's faster, or you're alone, or help is a few seconds away. Then your job isn't to win the ball immediately. It's to buy time. There's a five-step sequence for that. Say it with me. [pause] [slows down] Protect. Slow. Show. Wait. Win.

Protect means staying between the attacker and the most dangerous route to goal. Slow means taking away her speed. Show means steering her toward less dangerous space, usually away from the center. Wait means staying balanced, and not diving in just because the ball is nearby. And win means attacking the ball when the moment comes: her touch is poor, she loses her balance, help arrives, the space disappears, or the challenge turns in your favor.

An excellent delay may not contain a tackle at all. The attacker simply runs out of good options.

Now picture a different moment. An attacker has broken past you, and she's heading toward our goal. Most players just chase her back, running straight at where she is. That turns it into a footrace from behind, and footraces from behind are hard to win.

Instead, don't run to where the attacker is. Run to where she is going.

Aim first for the dangerous lane between her and the goal, then curve your run as the gap closes. That cuts off the middle, shortens your own route, and pushes her wider, away from danger. Different picture, same idea as delaying: you're protecting the most dangerous space first.

Let's go back to movement with the ball, because timing is everything.

Picture a teammate about to pass to someone else. Most players wait to see where the ball goes, then start moving. By then, the defender has already reacted. Instead, anticipate the next picture while the ball is traveling. Start moving as the pass is played, not after it arrives.

And once you've passed, you're not finished. Ask yourself, every time: Where can I help next? Then go there.

Remember: move on the pass, and move again after you pass.

There's a subtle part to this. Arrive, don't camp. If you get to a useful space too early, defenders have time to settle and block the lane. It's often better to move into the space just as the passing lane opens.

And watch your height compared to nearby teammates. If three of you stand in a flat line across the field, one defender can block all of you. Stagger instead, some higher up the field and some deeper. That creates angles, and it creates options for a third player.

On defense, your eyes have a different job.

You're gathering information about the ball, the goal, your teammates, your opponents, and dangerous space. It's easy to lock onto one player and follow her everywhere. But if you only see her, you'll miss the runner arriving behind you. See the ball, and the most dangerous space or opponent.

Protect dangerous space first. Get tighter as the ball and the danger get closer.

So get compact as a team first, then pick up the dangerous opponent in your part of the field. And there are moments to get tight right away: restarts, like free kicks, corners, and throw-ins; crosses; and any time you step to win the ball.

Last, the link between attacking and defending.

Picture yourself defending on the right side. You win the ball. If you were standing somewhere useless for attacking, the chance is gone before you can use it. But if your defensive position was also a good launching spot, you're off immediately.

Good defensive positioning should help the next attack. Good attacking positioning should help the next defensive transition. Defend from a position you can attack from.

When you put all of this together, it comes down to one standard. Good movement makes your teammates' decisions easier. Create an angle. Meet the ball. Stay balanced. Move again.
