# The High-Level Player Sheet

Let's start with the short version of everything. The rest of this audiobook goes deeper, but if you only kept one chapter in your head, this is the one to keep.

You don't need to memorize all of it today. You do need to know the four team mantras. Everything else you'll learn over time, one practice and one game at a time.

So let's start there.

We say our mantras out loud, together. The practice mantra is the first thing we say at every practice, and the game mantra is the first thing we say in every game warm-up. That can feel a little strange at first. But words you say out loud, together, are the words that come back to you when the game gets loud.

Say the practice mantra with me. [pause] [slows down] Scan. Talk. Intention. Faster.

Each of those words has a job. Scanning is collecting information before you need it: a quick look over your shoulder before the ball arrives, not after. Talking is giving a teammate information she can't see for herself, like the defender closing in on her blind side. Intention means every action has a reason. You're not just getting rid of the ball. You're putting it somewhere, for a purpose.

Faster comes last on purpose. It doesn't mean rushing. It means pushing the speed at which you can still do the first three correctly. Scan, talk, and play with intention at a comfortable pace. Then do it a little quicker. Then quicker again. That's what practice is for.

Say the game mantra with me. [pause] [slows down] Calm mind. Fast soccer.

This is where a lot of players get confused, because fast soccer sounds like getting rid of the ball quickly. It isn't.

Picture this. You receive in midfield. You checked your shoulder before the ball arrived, so you already know nobody is close. A panicked player would smash the ball forward the instant it touched her foot. But you know you have time. So you take an extra second, let a teammate finish her run, and then play the pass.

That's fast soccer. You saw early, decided quickly, and executed with control. Sometimes fast soccer means holding the ball. Sometimes it means playing backward. Sometimes it means carrying fifteen yards because nobody stepped to you.

The speed is in your eyes and your decisions. Not just your feet.

The third mantra is for after mistakes. Say the next-play mantra with me. [pause] [slows down] Respond. Reflect. Learn. Next play.

If you play creative, brave soccer, you will make mistakes. That isn't a warning. It's a promise. You'll try a turn and lose the ball. You'll attempt a pass that gets cut out. Those moments are for learning. They are never a reason for shame or embarrassment.

And the fourth is for pressure moments. Say the pressure mantra with me. [pause] [slows down] Want the moment.

Nerves are normal. That tight feeling before a penalty or a big moment is your body getting ready. It doesn't mean you aren't ready. Feel it, and step forward anyway.

Now let's talk about how we want to play.

Think about the moments in a game when you're farthest from the ball. Maybe it's on the other side of the field. It's easy to relax there, to stand and watch. Most players do.

We don't. Our standard is no hiding, no watching, influence the game. And there's one question that goes with it, a question you can ask yourself at any moment, with or without the ball: Where could I go right now that would make me useful?

Think back to your last game. [pause] Was there a moment when you were watching instead of moving? And where could you have gone? [long pause]

When we have the ball, every player is a possible outlet. If you aren't giving the ball carrier an option, you're making her problem harder, even if you never touch the ball.

So move on the pass. The moment a teammate plays the ball, you start moving toward the next useful space, not after the ball arrives. And once you've passed, don't stop to admire it. Move again.

Spacing matters too. Picture our team with the ball on the right side of the field. If everyone drifts toward the ball, the field shrinks, and the other team only has to defend one small area. So some players stay wide, some give depth, and nearby teammates stand at different heights, so passing lanes open at angles instead of along flat lines. The players near the ball pull defenders in. The players far from it get ready for the moment the ball comes their way.

Remember: strong side attracts. Weak side prepares.

On the ball, look in every direction: diagonal, sideways, backward, and forward. Forward is tempting, but forward only makes sense when we have an advantage there. When we do, go. When we don't, keep the ball and build one. And try to be hard to read. Show one thing, and do another. Look like you're changing your mind.

Now for one of the most important ideas in this whole handbook.

Rondos teach us how to survive when the game gets small: tight space, quick touches, defenders everywhere. That's a real skill. But soccer also asks you to recognize when the game gets big.

Picture this. You're squeezed near the touchline, and your team combines its way out of pressure with three quick passes. Suddenly you're facing forward, there's open grass in front of you, and the defense hasn't recovered yet.

What do a lot of young teams do next? They keep playing five-yard passes, as if they were still trapped. And a few seconds later, they are trapped again.

When you escape pressure, the problem has changed. So look up. Carry the ball into that open grass. Switch the field. Find a runner. Attack before the defense recovers. A longer pass in that moment isn't a panicked clearance. A long pass with a clear purpose is a good possession decision. You're looking for the biggest useful space.

Remember: small space, combine. Big space, exploit.

The goal isn't to pass short, and it isn't to pass long. The goal is to read the space in front of you and choose the right solution.

Without the ball, the thinking is similar. Protect the dangerous space first, and get tighter to opponents as the ball and the danger get closer. Protect the middle. And if an attacker is running at you and your help hasn't arrived, delay her instead of diving in.

When we lose the ball, decide right away. If we're close enough to win it back immediately, counterpress. If not, recover into shape. Either way, the first steps happen instantly.

Two more habits belong here. Defend from a position you can attack from, so that when we win the ball back, you're already useful. And head the ball with purpose, not out of habit.

Now, brave soccer.

We want you to turn with the ball, carry it, dribble, disguise your passes, play through pressure, combine with teammates, and try creative solutions when you see the chance.

Here's the deal we make with you. We will correct careless decisions. But we will not punish intelligent ideas simply because they didn't work.

So what's careless? Careless means ignoring the picture in front of you, or ignoring what we've worked on in training. Picture a turn in midfield. You checked your shoulder, you saw space, you went for it, and it didn't come off. That was an intelligent idea. Now picture the same turn as the last defender, with an attacker behind you that you never looked for. That's careless.

Same move. Different picture.

Think of a brave idea you tried that didn't work. [pause] Were you reading the picture, or ignoring it? [long pause]

During a game, you don't analyze the mistake. You respond, and you play the next action. The reflecting comes later: at a break, after the game, or on film. We'll walk through exactly how to do that in the next chapter.

Come back to the pressure mantra for a moment, because there's more to it.

The goal isn't to stop feeling fear. You probably can't, and you don't need to. The goal is to stop fear from making the decision for you.

At your age, the real loss isn't missing a penalty. The real loss is avoiding the chance to find out how you respond under pressure. We should fear wasting developmental opportunities more than we fear temporary failure.

You might be nervous. You might miss. Neither one is shameful.

But wanting the moment never means playing through pain or hiding an injury. If something hurts, tell a parent and your coach. That isn't a lack of courage. It's being responsible.

So where does winning fit in?

Winning isn't the main measure of your development. But competing to win is part of development, because caring about the result makes you concentrate harder, work harder, and recover harder. So want to win. Just play for the next action, not the scoreboard. A player who's staring at the scoreboard is a step slow on the next play.

Leadership isn't only for captains. Every player can lead, and not every leader is loud. One player organizes the back line with her voice. Another sets the effort standard in every drill. Another is always first to pick up a teammate after a mistake. Different styles, same idea: see what the team needs, and help provide it.

You also have a coach who's with you for every moment of every game. It's you.

Be coachable, and learn to coach yourself. In a game, self-coaching is a loop. You scan, you understand what you see, you decide, you execute, you reflect on how it went, and you adjust. Then around again.

And after every practice, ask yourself one question: What soccer problem did today's practice teach me to solve? [pause] What was the answer from your last practice? [long pause]

How we treat each other matters just as much.

Your effort is part of your teammate's training. If you defend half-heartedly in a drill, the attacker across from you isn't practicing against a real defender. So we compete hard against each other, to make each other better, not to rank each other.

When you help a teammate, help her learn. Don't grade her. Describe what you saw instead of judging it, and ask before you give advice. Use our shared team language. And when you praise someone, be specific. "Great scan before that turn" is worth far more than "nice job."

After a teammate's mistake, your body language counts. No sighs, no eye-rolls. During games, give information and encouragement, and save the advice for later. And leave selection, discipline, and major corrections to the coach.

Last, your own development.

At any point in the season, you should be able to name three things: one current strength to build on, one development priority you've chosen with your coach, and what progress on that priority would look like. Those will change as you improve. That's the point.

Can you name all three right now? [long pause] If any of them were hard to find, that's a great conversation to have with your coach.

It helps to keep two ideas separate. Your team role is where you help the team today. Your development role is the complete player you're becoming. You might play center back this season and still be becoming a player who can receive, turn, and combine anywhere on the field.

And if you want a different role or more minutes, ask the coach yourself: What would I need to show to earn that? You can talk during office hours at the field, or send a film question through the team app, with a parent included.

That's the short version. Everything that follows builds on it.
