# Injuries, Recovery, and Return to Play

Let's talk about something every athlete deals with sooner or later: getting hurt.

Injuries are part of sports. That isn't meant to scare you. We want to be prepared for them without becoming afraid of them. So this chapter is about what to do when something hurts, how to look after a teammate who's injured, and how to come back when it's your turn to return.

Underneath all of it is one idea. This team cares about people more than it cares about who's available for the next game.

Picture this. It's the second half of a big game. You plant to cut, and something in your ankle doesn't feel right. It isn't terrible. You could probably keep going. And there's a voice in your head saying: don't come off, you'll lose your spot, the team needs you, don't look weak.

That voice is wrong.

Your long-term health matters more than any practice, tournament, league game, or season. You should never feel pressure to hide an injury because you're afraid of losing playing time, letting teammates down, or looking weak. Telling someone honestly is responsible. Following your treatment and rehab is responsible. Taking the time you need to recover is responsible.

So how do you tell normal soreness from something that matters?

Sore muscles the day after a hard or new workout are common, and they ease with rest. Pain that starts while you're playing is different. Stop and tell a parent and your coach if something starts to hurt during play, especially an old injury. Stop if you notice you're limping, or moving differently to protect it. Stop if it's swollen, if you can't move it normally, or if the pain is very bad, getting worse, or not getting better with rest.

Some signs mean it's time to see a doctor: a limp, swelling, not being able to move it normally, very bad pain, or pain or swelling that gets worse or hasn't gone away after a few days.

Head injuries need their own conversation.

Picture a collision going up for a header. You get back up. You feel a little dazed, maybe dizzy, maybe just not quite right. Every instinct says shake it off and keep playing.

Don't. After any blow to the head or body, tell your coach right away, and come out if you feel dazed, dizzy, or "not right."

Here's what to watch for, in yourself or in a teammate. Feeling dazed or confused. Being slow to answer, or not remembering what just happened. Being unsteady, clumsier than usual, or suddenly different in mood. A headache, dizziness, nausea, trouble seeing, or being bothered by light or noise. Or simply feeling foggy.

Any one of those, and you come out.

Say it with me. [pause] [slows down] When in doubt, sit out.

Never try to shake it off. And if you see those signs in a teammate, tell a coach, even if she says she's fine.

A concussion follows its own step-by-step plan, led by your health care provider. The first day or two means rest, light walking, and less screen time. Then you move up one step at a time: light exercise, then soccer drills without contact, then full training. There's no contact, no heading, and no full training until you're cleared. Most teens recover within a month.

Since we're on the subject, let's talk about heading.

Head the ball with purpose, not out of habit. You don't have to attack every high ball just because it can be headed.

And here's something that surprises a lot of players. In heading situations, most concussions don't come from the ball. They come from contact with another player.

So when heading is the right choice, commit to it. Make an early call and a committed jump, or leave it. A late, half-hearted jump is exactly how collisions happen. Use your arms for balance and space, never your elbows. And never jump into a teammate's or an opponent's back.

Now come back to that voice in your head in the second half, the one telling you not to look weak. It has the idea of toughness backwards.

We respect tough athletes. But toughness doesn't mean ignoring an injury. Toughness can mean telling the truth when something hurts. It can mean accepting that you won't play for a while. It can mean doing rehab exercises consistently, week after week, staying mentally engaged while you're out, supporting teammates while you recover, dealing patiently with setbacks, and returning with courage when you're ready.

Which of those would be hardest for you? [long pause]

So we never pressure an injured teammate to prove her toughness by playing before she's ready. And a player who plays through something she should have reported is not our model of toughness.

Here's a promise from your coaches. Telling us early is never held against you. Sitting out while you're hurt is not a punishment. Coaches will thank you for speaking up, every time.

What happens after a meaningful injury?

Your parents will let the coach know about any medical restrictions, what recovery looks like, and when you can start practicing again. The coach doesn't make medical decisions. Those belong to you, your family, and your health care providers. The coach's job is to respect those limits completely, and to plan your training and game minutes within them.

You also get a voice in your own plan. The details of your injury belong to you and your family, and the coach shares with the team only what you and your family agree to.

Now switch places. This time, it's a teammate who's hurt.

Picture a friend on the team who got injured last week. She's at home, missing practice. It's easy for her to fade from the group without anyone meaning for it to happen. Practice goes on. The group chat moves on. And she starts to feel like she isn't really part of the team anymore.

You can stop that with a thirty-second message. "Hope you're doing okay. We miss you and can't wait to have you back." Ask how she's doing. Tell her you're thinking about her. Keep including her.

Is there a teammate who's out right now? Who could you message today? [long pause]

When it's comfortable for her, an injured player can stay connected in lots of ways: coming to games or practices, joining team meetings, watching film, helping spot tactical problems, encouraging teammates, learning a new position or concept, or just staying part of the team socially. But none of that should become an obligation when she needs rest, treatment, school time, or space. The goal is connection, not guilt.

And don't let the injury become her whole identity. Imagine every conversation starting with "How's the knee?" or "When are you coming back?" Check in, of course. But also talk to her normally, about school, about the weekend, about anything. She's still the same teammate.

Injuries can be hard emotionally, too.

Feeling sad, angry, frustrated, or left out after an injury is common. But if those feelings aren't easing or are getting worse, or you're pulling away from friends, or your sleep or eating changes a lot, tell a parent. Talking with a counselor or doctor is part of recovery, just like rehab is.

And this part matters a lot. If you ever think about hurting yourself, or a teammate talks about hurting herself, get help right away. Call or text nine, eight, eight. In an emergency, call nine, one, one.

Now, coming back.

You'd think the hard part of an injury would be over the day you're cleared to play. For a lot of athletes, it isn't. Being medically recovered and feeling completely fearless aren't always the same thing.

Picture your first practice back after a knee injury. The drill calls for a sharp cut. Your knee has healed, but your body hesitates anyway. You slow down before you plant. You pull out of a tackle you would have made before.

That doesn't mean you lack courage. Hesitating during contact, sprinting, cutting, jumping, tackling, striking the ball, or repeating the movement that hurt you is really common. So is feeling nervous and excited at the same time. Confidence often has to be rebuilt through successful repetitions, one at a time. And if the fear isn't fading after you've been cleared, tell a parent, so your provider knows.

The return itself moves step by step, from controlled situations toward full competition. A typical path, set with your health care provider, goes like this: controlled work on your own, then team drills without contact, then full training, then short game minutes, then full games.

You move up when you handle each step well, not just because a date on the calendar arrives. If pain or swelling comes back, you step back, and your family checks with your provider. Stepping back is part of the process, not a failure. And short minutes at first are part of the plan, not a judgment about you.

Some injuries take much longer. After a serious knee injury, like a torn ACL, getting back to a cutting and pivoting sport like soccer often takes a year or more, and your medical team sets that timeline. The chance of another ACL injury is highest in the first year after surgery. So waiting until you're truly ready isn't the soft choice. It's the tough one.

Coming back, you may need to rebuild a lot: movement confidence, timing, fitness, comfort on the ball, decision speed, confidence in contact, and trust in the injured area. So the question isn't just "Can I survive a game?" The better question is "Can I play freely and confidently again?"

Once you've recovered and you're cleared, we want you to trust yourself again, gradually. An injury shouldn't permanently teach you fear. Eventually, you want to be able to think: "I am prepared. I am strong. I can play normally again."

Teammates have a big part in that return.

Don't question why a teammate is still out, or tell her she should be playing. Don't tease her about being injured. Don't compare recovery times either. Two injuries can sound alike and still heal on completely different schedules. And don't pass on details or guesses about her injury, in person or online.

When she comes back, welcome her back normally. Don't treat her as fragile once she's ready, and don't keep reminding her she was hurt. Picture her first practice back: she mistimes a tackle, or loses a sprint she would have won a month ago. Don't turn that moment into evidence she came back too soon. She's finding her rhythm again. Support her, encourage her, and welcome her back.

And if you're the one returning, lean on the same principles you used before you got hurt.

Say the practice mantra with me. [pause] [slows down] Scan. Talk. Intention. Faster.

Say the game mantra with me. [pause] [slows down] Calm mind. Fast soccer.

And when that first difficult physical moment arrives, the first real tackle or the first hard cut, say the pressure mantra with me. [pause] [slows down] Want the moment.

Not because you should ignore fear or pain. But because once you're healthy, cleared, and ready, that moment is part of rebuilding your confidence.

So here's the standard for this team.

We care about our teammates when they're playing. We care about them when they're injured. We care about them while they recover. And we care enough about them not to rush them back.

Remember: health first. Stay connected. Recover completely. Return confidently.
