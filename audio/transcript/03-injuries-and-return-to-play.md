# Injuries, Recovery, and Return to Play

Injuries are part of sports. We should prepare for them without becoming afraid of them. Our goals are to protect your health, support you emotionally when you're hurt, keep you connected to the team, communicate clearly, respect medical guidance, and help you return without unnecessary fear. We want a team culture that cares about people more than about who's available for the next game.

Health comes first.

Your long-term health matters more than a practice, a tournament, a league game, or a season. You should never feel pressure to hide an injury because you're afraid of losing playing time, disappointing teammates, or looking weak. Reporting an injury honestly is responsible. Following treatment and rehab is responsible. Taking the time you need to recover is responsible.

Quick check. [pause] Sore legs the day after a hard workout, or pain that starts while you're playing. Which one means stop and tell someone? [long pause] Pain that starts while you play.

Sore muscles the day after a hard or new workout are common, and they ease with rest. Pain that starts while you play is different. Stop and tell a parent and your coach if something starts to hurt while you play, especially an old injury. Stop if you're limping or moving differently to protect it, if it's swollen or you can't move it normally, or if the pain is very bad, getting worse, or not getting better with rest.

A limp, swelling, not being able to move it normally, very bad pain, or pain or swelling that gets worse or doesn't go away after a few days means see a doctor.

Head injuries.

After a blow to the head or body, tell your coach right away. Come out if you feel dazed, dizzy, or "not right."

Here are the signs to watch for, in yourself or in a teammate. [pause] Feeling dazed or confused, being slow to answer, or not remembering what just happened. [short pause] Being unsteady, clumsier than usual, or having a change in mood. [short pause] A headache, dizziness, nausea, vision problems, or being bothered by light or noise. [short pause] Or just feeling foggy, or not right.

Any of these, and you come out. Say it with me. [pause] [slows down] When in doubt, sit out.

Never try to shake it off. And if you see these signs in a teammate, tell a coach.

A concussion follows its own step-by-step plan, led by your health care provider. The first day or two means rest, light walking, and less screen time. Then you move up one step at a time: light exercise, then soccer drills without contact, then full training. There's no contact, no heading, and no full training until you're cleared. Most teens recover within a month.

Heading with purpose.

Head the ball with purpose, not habit. You don't have to attack every high ball just because it can be headed.

Here's a question. [pause] In heading situations, what do you think causes most concussions: the ball, or something else? [long pause] Most come from contact with another player, not from the ball.

So when heading is the right choice, make an early call and a committed jump, or leave it. No late, half-hearted jumps. Use your arms for balance and space, never your elbows. And never jump into a teammate's or an opponent's back.

What toughness means.

We respect the toughness of athletes. But toughness does not mean ignoring an injury. Toughness can mean telling the truth when something hurts. It can mean accepting that you may be unable to play for a while, doing your rehab consistently, and staying mentally engaged while you're injured. It can mean supporting teammates while you recover, dealing patiently with setbacks, and returning with courage when you're ready.

Which of those kinds of toughness would be hardest for you? [long pause]

Respect the work an injured teammate is doing to return. Never pressure her to prove her toughness by playing before she's ready.

Telling us early is never held against you. Sitting out while you're hurt is not a punishment. Coaches will thank you for speaking up, every time. A player who plays through something she should have reported is not the model of toughness for this team.

After an injury.

After a meaningful injury, your parents will let the coach know about any medical restrictions, what recovery looks like, and when you can start practicing again. The coach doesn't make medical decisions. Those belong to you, your family, and your health care providers. The coach's job is to respect those limits completely, and to plan your training and game minutes within them.

You should have a voice in your own plan. The details of your injury belong to you and your family. The coach shares with the team only what you and your family agree to.

Take care of your teammates.

When a teammate gets hurt, let her know she matters beyond what she does on the field. Send her a message. Ask how she's doing. Tell her you're thinking about her, and that you're looking forward to having her back. Keep including her.

A simple message like "Hope you're doing okay. We miss you and can't wait to have you back" can mean a great deal. Nobody should disappear socially from the team just because she can't play for a while.

The injured player is still part of the team.

When it's appropriate and comfortable, injured players stay connected. That might mean coming to games or practices, joining team meetings, watching film, helping spot tactical problems, encouraging teammates, learning a new position or concept, or just staying part of the team socially.

None of this should become an obligation when you need rest, treatment, school time, or emotional space. The goal is connection, not guilt.

Don't make the injury her identity. Ask about the player, not only the injury. An injured athlete can quickly feel like every conversation starts with "How's the knee?" or "When are you coming back?" Check in, but also talk to her normally. She's still the same teammate.

Feeling sad, angry, frustrated, or left out after an injury is common. If those feelings aren't easing or are getting worse, or if you pull away from friends, or your sleep or eating changes a lot, tell a parent. Talking with a counselor or doctor is part of recovery, just like rehab. And if you ever think about hurting yourself, or a teammate talks about hurting herself, get help right away: call or text nine, eight, eight, or call nine, one, one in an emergency.

Returning can be mentally difficult.

Being medically recovered and feeling completely fearless are not always the same thing. When you come back, you might hesitate during contact, sprinting, cutting, jumping, tackling, striking the ball, or repeating the movement that hurt you.

That doesn't mean you lack courage. Feeling nervous and excited at the same time is common. Confidence may need to be rebuilt through successful repetitions. If fear isn't fading after you're cleared, tell a parent so your provider knows.

Return step by step.

The return moves from controlled situations toward full competition. A typical path, set with your health care provider, looks like this: controlled work on your own, then team drills without contact, then full training, then short game minutes, then full games. You move up when you handle each step well, not just because a date arrives. If pain or swelling comes back, you step back, and your family checks with your provider. Stepping back is part of the process, not a failure. Short minutes at first are part of the plan, not a judgment.

After a serious knee injury, like a torn ACL, returning to a cutting and pivoting sport like soccer often takes a year or more, and your medical team sets the timeline. The chance of another ACL injury is highest in the first year after surgery, so waiting until you're truly ready is the tough choice.

You may need to rebuild movement confidence, timing, fitness, comfort on the ball, decision speed, confidence in contact, and trust in the injured area. The goal is not simply, "Can I survive a game?" The better question is, "Can I play freely and confidently again?"

Don't be afraid of the game.

Once you've recovered and you're cleared to return, we want you to trust yourself again, gradually. We don't want an injury to permanently teach fear. Eventually, you should be able to think: [slows down] "I am prepared. I am strong. I can play normally again."

Respect a teammate's return.

Don't question why a teammate is still out. Don't tell her she should be playing. Don't tease her about being injured. Don't compare recovery times, and don't assume that because two injuries sound similar, the recovery should be the same. And don't pass on details or guesses about her injury, in person or online. Instead: support, encourage, and welcome her back.

When a teammate returns, welcome her back normally. Don't treat her as fragile once she's ready, and don't keep reminding her she was injured. Don't turn her first mistake, missed tackle, lost sprint, or moment of hesitation into evidence she came back too soon. She may need time to find her rhythm.

If you're the one returning, focus on the same principles you used before.

The team standard.

We care about our teammates when they're playing. We care about them when they're injured. We care about them while they recover. And we care enough about them not to rush them back.
